Nature of matter, solid-liquid-gas states, phase transitions, latent heat, evaporation, and diffusion

🔍
Quick Reference
Key Facts and Contributions
Nature Of Matter 1
State of Matter / Phase ChangeKey Characteristic or ProcessExamples / Thermal Effect
Solid StateDefinite shape and definite volume; negligible compressibility, extremely strong intermolecular forces, and minimum intermolecular spaceIron, ice, wood, copper; high density and high rigidity
Liquid StateIndefinite shape but definite volume; fluidity (ability to flow), intermolecular forces weaker than in solids, and particle mobilityWater, milk, oil, mercury; takes the shape of the container and exhibits surface tension
Gaseous StateIndefinite shape and indefinite volume; highest compressibility, minimum intermolecular attraction, and rapid random motion of moleculesOxygen, nitrogen, air, CNG, LPG; spreads uniformly throughout the entire available volume
Plasma StateSuper-energetic and super-excited ionized gas (free electrons and positive ions); an excellent conductor of electricitySun and stars (at high temperatures of nuclear fusion), fluorescent tubes, neon sign bulbs, and lightning
Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC)Super-cooling a gas of extremely low density near absolute zero (-273.15°C / 0 K) so all atoms occupy a single quantum stateSatyendra Nath Bose and Albert Einstein (1920); experimentally demonstrated on rubidium in 1995 by Cornell, Wieman, and Ketterle
SublimationDirect conversion of a solid into the gaseous state without passing through the liquid state; an endothermic processCamphor, naphthalene balls, ammonium chloride (sal ammoniac), iodine, dry ice (solid CO₂)
Quick Reference
Key Facts and Contributions
Nature Of Matter 1
State of Matter / Phase ChangeKey Characteristic or ProcessExamples / Thermal Effect
Latent Heat of FusionHeat required to convert 1 kg of a solid into liquid at its melting point without changing temperature (for ice: 3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg or 80 cal/g)Ice at 0°C provides more effective cooling than water at the same temperature
Latent Heat of VaporizationHeat required to convert 1 kg of a liquid into vapour at its boiling point without changing temperature (for water: 2.26 × 10⁶ J/kg or 540 cal/g)Burns caused by steam at 100°C are far more severe and painful than burns from boiling water at 100°C
Evaporation CoolingEscape of molecules from the surface of a liquid into vapour at any temperature below its boiling point; produces cooling by drawing latent heat from surroundingsWater staying cool in an earthen pot (matka), cooling when sweat dries, evaporation of acetone/sanitizer on the palm
DiffusionSpontaneous intermixing of particles from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration due to their own motion; faster at higher temperatures due to increased kinetic energyAroma of hot food spreading far, fragrance of perfume, dissolving of potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) in water
DepositionDirect conversion of a gas into the solid state without passing through the liquid state; an exothermic process (reverse of sublimation)Formation of frost in winter, soot deposition in chimneys, formation of snow crystals in the atmosphere
Pressure and Boiling Point RelationIncreasing external atmospheric pressure raises the boiling point, while decreasing pressure lowers the boiling pointIn a pressure cooker, water boils at 120°C under high pressure (faster cooking); at high altitudes, lower pressure causes water to boil at 70–80°C
