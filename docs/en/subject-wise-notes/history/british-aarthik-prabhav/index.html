Land Revenue Systems
Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari, and Mahalwari Systems — Core Basis & Extent
Comparative Overview of the Three Major Land Revenue Systems of British India
System & Exponent Area Covered & Share of Total Land (%) Revenue Payer & Key Features
Permanent Settlement (Lord Cornwallis, 1793) Bengal, Bihar, Odisha, Banaras, and Northern Karnataka (19% of British India's area) Zamindars made permanent proprietors of land; revenue assessment fixed in perpetuity; 10/11 share to the Company and 1/11 to the Zamindar; Sunset Law enforced
Ryotwari System (Thomas Munro & Captain Read, 1820) Madras, Bombay, Assam, and Coorg (51% of British India's area) Peasant (Ryot) recognized as the actual proprietor of land; direct legal relationship with the government; tax fixed at 1/3 to 1/2 of produce; rates revised every 30 years
Mahalwari System (Holt Mackenzie & Martin Bird, 1822/1833) United Provinces (Awadh/Agra), Central Provinces, and Punjab (30% of British India's area) Entire village community (Mahal) held collectively responsible for revenue; collection through village headman (Lambardar); exorbitant tax rates of 50% to 66%

Comparative Analysis
Comparative Table of the Three Major Land Revenue Systems
Land Relations, Revenue Assessment, Tenure, and Historical Impact on Peasants
System & Land Ownership Revenue Assessment & Revision Period Impact on Peasants and the Rural Economy
Permanent Settlement (Landowner: Zamindar) Fixed permanently based on the 1790 revenue collection; no future revision; 10/11 share to the Company Peasants stripped of all proprietary rights; rise of absentee landlordism and peak exploitation; Zamindars supported the British in 1857
Ryotwari System (Landowner: Peasant) Based on land survey and soil fertility; 33% to 50% of produce; rates revised every 20 to 30 years The exploitative state itself replaced the Zamindar; coercive collection; land confiscation even during droughts and famines; deeper entrapment under moneylenders
Mahalwari System (Landowner: Village Community) 50% to 66% of the estimated income of the entire village; steep periodic increases in rates Disintegration of traditional joint village institutions; dominance of Lambardars; excessive rent rendered peasants landless

Impact of Colonial Economic Policies Across Sectors
Effect of British Rule on Agriculture, Handicrafts, Industry, Trade, and Capital
Economic Sector British Colonial Policy & Nature Actual Impact on the Indian Economy
Agriculture Sector Excessive land revenue demand; strict confiscation rules; forced cultivation of cash crops; private property in land Unintended commercialization of agriculture; crippling rural indebtedness; frequent devastating famines; widespread rural impoverishment
Handicrafts & Cottage Industries Zero or minimal import duty on British manufactured goods; prohibitive tariffs on Indian goods in Britain Complete ruin of traditional handloom and metal crafts (De-industrialization); mass migration of millions of artisans to agriculture
Trade & Commerce Free Trade policy (1813); monopoly of British shipping; colonial control over internal customs posts India reduced to an importer of finished goods and exporter of raw materials; Britain captured the gains of a favourable balance of trade
Modern Industry & Capital Lack of tariff protection for nascent Indian industries; 5% guaranteed return and railway concessions for foreign capital Foreign control through the Managing Agency system; limited and delayed growth of Indian industrialists

Drain of Wealth
Drain of Wealth Theory — Key Exponents, Arguments & Evidence
Economic Analysis of the Unrequited Transfer of Wealth from India to England
Thinker / Exponent Major Work & Year Presented Core Arguments & Uncovered Evidence
Dadabhai Naoroji ('Grand Old Man of India') 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India' (1901); Paper: 'England's Debt to India' (1867) First to propound the Drain Theory; termed it the 'Evil of all Evils'; identified Home Charges, interest on foreign capital, and British officials' remittances as pillars of exploitation
Romesh Chunder Dutt (R. C. Dutt) 'The Economic History of India' (1901–1906, two volumes) Compared British revenue policy to a sponge that 'draws up all the good things from the banks of the Ganges, and squeezes them down on the banks of the Thames'; argued that half of total revenue was drained
Mahadev Govind Ranade 'Essays on Indian Economics' (1898) Analysed the continuous drain of over one-third of India's national income; demonstrated India's complete colonial economic dependence
William Digby 'Prosperous British India' (1901) Proved using official statistics that Indian poverty and starvation multiplied in the late 19th century due to colonial plunder
Indian National Congress Calcutta Session (1896; President: Rahimatullah M. Sayani) Formally adopted Naoroji's 'Drain of Wealth Theory'; declared it the root cause of India's poverty
