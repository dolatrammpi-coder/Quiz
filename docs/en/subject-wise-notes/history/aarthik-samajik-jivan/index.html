Economic Institutions
Major Types of Land Grants, Terminology, and Their Nature
Classification of early medieval rural economy and land rights
Land Grant / Technical Term Nature of Grant & Holder Social, Administrative & Economic Impact
Brahmadeya Tax-free land grant given individually to learned Brahmanas Rise of Brahmanas as a landowning class; expansion of agriculture into new regions
Agrahara Tax-free land granted collectively to an entire village or group of Brahmanas Creation of autonomous rural settlements; strengthening of Vedic education and temple culture
Devadana / Devadeya Permanent grant for the daily worship and maintenance of temples, mathas, and shrines Transformation of temples into powerful economic banks and massive landowning centers
Pallichchandam Land grant given to Jain temples, basadis, and Buddhist viharas Economic promotion and institutional patronage of non-Brahmanical Shramana sects
Bhoga / Jivika Revenue-bearing land assigned to military and administrative officials in lieu of salary Growth of feudal sovereignty; administrative decentralization and erosion of central control
Bhumichhidravidhana Tax-free or concessional land granted to one who brings forest or barren land under cultivation Geographical expansion of arable land and integration of tribal areas into the mainstream

Philosophical Revival
Major Early Medieval Philosophers, Their Period, Philosophy, and Works
Leading Acharyas of Indian philosophy and their intellectual traditions
Acharya & Period Philosophy & Sect Major Texts & Historical Contribution
Adi Shankaracharya (788–820 CE) Advaita Vedanta (Smarta tradition) Shariraka Bhashya (Brahmasutra Bhashya), Gita Bhashya, Upanishad Bhashyas; commentaries on Prasthanatrayi and establishment of four Mathas in four directions
Ramanujacharya (1017–1137 CE) Vishishtadvaita (Sri Sampradaya) Sri Bhashya, Vedartha Sangraha, Gita Bhashya; doctrine of devotion (Bhakti) to Saguna God (Narayana) and Prapatti (self-surrender)
Madhvacharya (1199–1278 CE) Dvaitavada (Brahma Sampradaya) Anubhashya, Tattvaviveka; eternal and real distinction between Jiva (soul) and Ishvara (God); Vishnu as the supreme independent reality
Nimbarkacharya (12th Century) Dvaitadvaita (Bhedabheda) (Sanaka Sampradaya) Vedanta Parijata Saurabha; coexistence of both difference and non-difference between Jiva and Brahman; worship of Radha-Krishna
Kumarila Bhatta & Prabhakara (8th Century) Purva Mimamsa Philosophy Shlokavarttika, Tantravarttika; philosophical re-establishment of the apaurusheya (uncreated) nature of the Vedas and Vedic rituals
Philosophical Comparison
Comparative Features of the Philosophies of Shankaracharya and Ramanujacharya
Advaita Vedanta vs. Vishishtadvaita — Metaphysics and Path to Liberation
Philosophical Basis / Element Adi Shankaracharya (Advaita Vedanta) Ramanujacharya (Vishishtadvaita)
Fundamental Nature of Brahman Nirguna (attributeless), formless, unqualified, and indescribable pure consciousness Saguna (with attributes), endowed with form and infinite auspicious qualities, Purushottama (Vishnu)
Reality of the World (Jagat) The world is an empirical appearance born of Maya (Avidya); illusory at the transcendental level The world is real and true; it is the direct body (prakara) of Brahman itself
Relation Between Jiva and Brahman Jiva and Brahman are fundamentally one (non-dual); 'Tat Tvam Asi', 'Aham Brahmasmi' Jiva is an inseparable part and body of Brahman, yet retains distinct individuality
Means of Attaining Moksha Pure knowledge (Jnana Marga); Self-realization through the destruction of Avidya is liberation Bhakti and Prapatti (self-surrender); attainment of Vaikuntha through divine grace
Social & Practical Outlook Renunciation and knowledge at the transcendental level; Varna order accepted at the empirical level Path of Bhakti accessible to all Varnas and Antyajas; spread of social harmony
