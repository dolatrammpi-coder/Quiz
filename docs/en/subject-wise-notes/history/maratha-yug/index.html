Pillars of Maratha Swarajya — Hill Forts, Sea Forts, and Capitals
Fort and Geographical Region Expansion and Construction/Capture Strategic and Historical Significance
Shivneri Fort (Junnar, Pune) Ancient Yadava-era hill fort; Shahaji's jagir Birthplace of Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj; center of early upbringing and education by mother Jijabai
Torna Fort (Prachandagad) (Pune District) Captured from Bijapur in 1646 CE at the age of just 16 Shivaji's first military victory; hidden treasure found at the fort laid the foundation for Swarajya's expansion
Rajgad Fort (Southwest of Pune) Built by Shivaji himself on Murumbdev hill Served as the first active capital of the Maratha Empire for nearly 25 years from 1647 to 1674 CE
Pratapgad Fort (Mahabaleshwar, Satara) Built in 1656 CE under the supervision of Moropant Pingle Historic site of Afzal Khan's slaying in 1659 CE; symbol of Maratha sovereignty against Bijapur
Raigad Fort (Rairi) (Konkan, Mahad) Captured from Chandrarao More in 1656 CE; expansive ramparts Site of Shivaji's grand coronation in 1674 CE; permanent capital of the Maratha Empire and Shivaji's samadhi
Sinhagad (Kondhana) (Near Pune) Recaptured from the Mughals by Tanaji Malusare in 1670 CE Shivaji's famous remark on Tanaji's sacrifice: 'Gad aala, pan Sinha gela' (The fort is won, but the lion is lost)
Sindhudurg and Vijaydurg (Konkan Coast) Impregnable naval sea forts built on Malvan rock Principal naval base of the Maratha Navy (Armada); checked the Portuguese, English, and Siddi pirates

Central Administration
Shivaji's Ashtapradhan and Their Functions/Departments
Apex 8-Member Administrative Advisory Council of the Maratha Polity
Office (Ashtapradhan) Functions and Responsibilities Administrative and Court Standing
Peshwa (Mukhya Pradhan) Conducted all administrative affairs, governed in the king's absence, and led the army Highest office in the Ashtapradhan; chief executive of the empire after the Chhatrapati
Amatya (Majumdar) Audited imperial income and expenditure, managed the finance department, and maintained annual accounts Equivalent to a modern Finance Minister; signature mandatory on all financial and revenue farmans
Waqia-Navis (Mantri) Maintained records of the king's daily routine, court proceedings, security arrangements, and personal spies Equivalent to a modern Home Minister; guardian of the king's daily life and court discipline
Shurunavis / Chitnis (Sachiv) Managed royal correspondence, drafted farmans, and examined pargana accounts Secretary for home and correspondence; affixed the 'Approved' (Sammat) seal on official orders
Sumant (Dabir) Handled foreign relations, received envoys, and advised diplomatically on war and treaties Equivalent to a modern Foreign Minister; gathered secret political intelligence from neighboring states
Sar-i-Naubat (Senapati) Oversaw recruitment, discipline, weaponry, logistics, and battlefield command of the entire army Supreme military commander; Hansaji Mohite (Hambirrao) and Prataprao Gujar were prominent Senapatis
Panditrao (Danadhyaksha) Oversaw religious rituals, distribution of grants to scholars and Brahmins, and regulation of social dharma Head of religious and cultural affairs; completely exempt from going to the battlefield
Nyayadhish Heard highest appeals in civil and criminal cases and dispensed justice based on Smriti laws Highest judicial authority after the Chhatrapati; also exempt from battlefield duty

Revenue System
Maratha Revenue System — Chauth, Sardeshmukhi, and Other Major Sources
System of Mobilizing Financial Resources from Swarajya and Neighboring Mughal/Deccani Territories
Tax / Revenue Source Rate and Basis of Assessment Collection Area and Impact
Land Revenue (Swarajya) Initially 33%, later 40%; land measured using Kathi; payable in both cash and kind Swarajya (core Maratha state); protection to peasants, taqavi loans, and pattas; suppression of intermediaries
Chauth 1/4th (25%) of the total revenue of neighboring states; protection guarantee levy Border territories of Mughal and Deccani sultanates; assurance of protection from Maratha raids and external attacks
Sardeshmukhi 1/10th (10%) of total revenue; legal claim as hereditary Sardeshmukh Maharashtra and 6 Mughal subas of the Deccan; basis for establishing the Chhatrapati's hereditary overlordship
Mokasa 66% share of Chauth assigned to Maratha sardars for maintaining their troops Jagirs and military expenses of Maratha commanders; later became an instrument of feudal expansion under the Peshwas
Babti and Sahotra Babti: 25% of Chauth (Chhatrapati's share); Sahotra: 6% of Chauth (Sachiv / Pant Sachiv's share) Central treasury fund and shares allocated for allowances of Ashtapradhan ministers

Chronology of Decisive Maratha Conflicts of the 17th and 18th Centuries
Battle or Treaty & Year Belligerents / Parties Key Events and Outcome

Peshwa Chronology
Prominent Peshwas, Their Tenure, and Major Political/Military Events
Historical Contribution of the 18th-Century Maratha Peshwas Operating from Pune
Peshwa and Tenure Major Military Campaigns and Policies Impact on the Maratha Empire
Balaji Vishwanath (1713–1720 CE) Supported Shahuji; Treaty of Delhi (1719); helped depose Farrukhsiyar; secured Chauth-Sardeshmukhi rights in the Deccan Made the Peshwa office hereditary; regarded as the second founder of the Maratha Empire; financial consolidation
Bajirao I (1720–1740 CE) 'Hindu Pad Padshahi'; defeated Nizam at Palkhed (1728) and Bhopal (1737); raid on Delhi (1737); Bassein victory (1739) Pan-Indian expansion of Maratha power; control over Malwa, Gujarat, and Bundelkhand; zenith of Ganimi Kava
Balaji Bajirao (Nana Saheb) (1740–1761 CE) Sangola Agreement (1750); Punjab-Attock conquest by Raghunathrao (1758); Battle of Udgir (1760); Third Battle of Panipat (1761) Peshwa became the de facto ruler; empire reached its territorial zenith, but heavy losses at Panipat initiated decline
Madhavrao I (1761–1772 CE) Defeated the Nizam of Hyderabad and Hyder Ali of Mysore; made the Mughals pensioners again in 1771 Healed the wounds of Panipat within 10 years; restored Maratha prestige; untimely death from tuberculosis was a severe blow
Bajirao II (1796–1818 CE) Incompetent and scheming; signed Subsidiary Alliance of Bassein with the British in 1802; Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–18) Complete dissolution of the Maratha Confederacy; Peshwaship abolished; exiled to Bithoor (Kanpur) on an 8-lakh pension
Imperial Expansion
Major Phases of Maratha Expansion and Key Regions
Rise of a Pan-Indian Power from Swarajya to Cuttack-to-Attock
Phase and Period of Expansion Key Regions and Leaders Political and Strategic Outcome
First Phase (1646–1680) [Establishment of Swarajya] Maharashtra, Konkan, Karnataka (Jinji-Vellore) Leadership: Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Foundation of independent Hindavi Swarajya against Bijapur and Mughals; hill forts and navy built; Raigad as capital
Second Phase (1680–1707) [War of Independence] Deccan, Jinji, Satara, and entire Maharashtra Leadership: Sambhaji, Rajaram, and Tarabai Successful resistance against Aurangzeb's 25-year Deccan siege; people's war exhausted and pushed back the Mughals
Third Phase (1707–1740) [March Toward North India] Malwa, Gujarat, Bundelkhand, Rajasthan border Leadership: Peshwa Bajirao I Direct challenge to Mughal supremacy; raids up to Delhi; broke out of the Deccan to establish dominance over Central India
Fourth Phase (1740–1761) [From Cuttack to Attock] Punjab (Attock), Odisha (Cuttack), Bengal, Delhi Leadership: Balaji Bajirao and Raghunathrao Mughal Emperor under Maratha protection; supreme political power in India; direct confrontation with Ahmad Shah Abdali

Third Battle of Panipat (1761 CE) — Parties, Leaders, Course, and Outcome
Ahmad Shah Abdali vs. Maratha Confederacy — Epoch-Making Battle of the 18th Century
Phase / Component of Battle Belligerents and Key Commanders Course of Events, Strategic Causes, and Outcome
Belligerents and Key Leaders Maratha Army (Sadashivrao Bhau, Vishwasrao, Ibrahim Gardi, Malharrao Holkar) vs. Afghan Coalition (Ahmad Shah Abdali, Najib-ud-Daulah, Shuja-ud-Daulah) Marathas stood isolated; Rajputs, Jats (Surajmal), and Sikhs remained neutral; Awadh and Rohilkhand united behind Abdali
Strategic Conditions and Supplies Marathas abandoned traditional Ganimi Kava for pitched battle; burdened by a huge non-combatant (pilgrim) train Abdali crossed the Yamuna and completely severed Maratha supply lines; severe starvation gripped the Maratha camp for 2 months
Battle of 14 January 1761 Initial fierce bombardment by Ibrahim Khan Gardi's artillery; Vishwasrao fatally shot in the head by afternoon Sadashivrao Bhau dismounted his elephant to fight on horseback and fell; Malharrao Holkar withdrew; rout of the Maratha army
Human and Material Losses 28,000 prominent Maratha warriors killed on the battlefield; Jankoji Scindia and Ibrahim Gardi captured and executed Hardly a family in Maharashtra was spared the loss of a relative; an entire generation perished
Historical and Long-Term Impact Maratha dream of a pan-Indian empire shattered forever; Peshwa Balaji Bajirao died of grief Created a power vacuum in India; paved an open path for the British East India Company from Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764)
