Decisive Battles
Battles of Plassey and Buxar — Key Parties, Causes, and Historical Outcomes
Comparative Overview of the Establishment of British Political Power in Bengal
Battle & Year Belligerents & Commanders Main Causes, Events & Decisive Outcomes
Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757) Siraj-ud-Daulah (Bengal) vs. Robert Clive (British) Causes: Fortification without the Nawab's permission and misuse of Dastak; betrayal by Mir Jafar and Rai Durlabh; Nawab defeated and killed; Mir Jafar made puppet Nawab; British received the Zamindari of 24 Parganas
Battle of Buxar (22 October 1764) Tripartite Alliance: Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daulah (Awadh), Shah Alam II (Mughal) vs. Hector Munro (British Army) Causes: Abolition of internal trade duties and fortification of Munger by Mir Qasim; decisive victory of Munro's disciplined army; Treaty of Allahabad in 1765; British obtained the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha

Phases of Imperial Expansion
Major Phases of British Territorial Expansion — 1757 to 1856
Expansion Starting from Bengal to the North-West, South India, and Punjab
Phase & Period Key Regions & Battles/Treaties Strategic Outcome & British Supremacy
First Phase (1757–1765) [Conquest of Bengal] Bengal, Bihar & Odisha; Plassey (1757), Buxar (1764), Treaty of Allahabad (1765) Transformation of a trading company into a sovereign ruler; control over vast financial resources (Diwani); laid the economic foundation for the conquest of India
Second Phase (1767–1799) [Conquest of South India] Mysore & Deccan; Four Anglo-Mysore Wars (1767–99); Treaty of Seringapatam (1792) End of the challenge from Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan; martyrdom of Tipu in 1799; undisputed British control over South India
Third Phase (1798–1818) [Marathas & Central India] Maratha Confederacy & Rajputana; Three Anglo-Maratha Wars; Treaty of Bassein (1802); Wellesley's Subsidiary Alliance Abolition of the Peshwa's office; complete elimination of Maratha power; British East India Company emerged as the paramount power across North and Central India
Fourth Phase (1843–1856) [North-West & Annexations] Sindh (1843), Punjab (1849) & Awadh (1856); Two Anglo-Sikh Wars; Dalhousie's Doctrine of Lapse Extension of India's geographical frontiers up to the Indus River and Khyber Pass; complete annexation of subsidiary states into direct British rule

Anglo-Indian Wars
Anglo-Mysore, Anglo-Maratha, and Anglo-Sikh Wars — Governors, Treaties, and Outcomes
Concise Account of Major Regional Conflicts in British Imperial Expansion
War & Period Governor-General & Belligerents Concluding Treaty & Decisive Outcome
First Anglo-Mysore War (1767–1769 CE) Governor: Verelst; Hyder Ali vs. British & Nizam Treaty of Madras (1769); diplomatic victory for Hyder Ali; British compelled to sign a defensive alliance
Second Anglo-Mysore War (1780–1784 CE) Governor-General: Warren Hastings; Hyder Ali & Tipu vs. Sir Eyre Coote Treaty of Mangalore (1784); death of Hyder Ali in 1782; mutual restitution of conquered territories
Third Anglo-Mysore War (1790–1792 CE) Governor-General: Lord Cornwallis; Tipu Sultan vs. British-Nizam-Maratha Alliance Treaty of Seringapatam (1792); Tipu lost half of his kingdom; two of his sons kept as hostages
Fourth Anglo-Mysore War (1799 CE) Governor-General: Lord Wellesley; Tipu Sultan vs. Wellesley & Harris No treaty; Tipu fell fighting on 4 May 1799; Subsidiary Alliance with the Wodeyar dynasty; complete control over Mysore
First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782 CE) Governor-General: Warren Hastings; Marathas (Nana Phadnavis) vs. British Treaty of Salbai (1782); 20 years of peace; Madhavrao Narayan recognized as Peshwa
Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805 CE) Governor-General: Lord Wellesley; Scindia, Bhonsle, Holkar vs. British Treaties of Deogaon (Bhonsle) and Surji-Anjangaon (Scindia); both accepted Subsidiary Alliance; capture of Delhi
Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1818 CE) Governor-General: Lord Hastings; Baji Rao II, Holkar & Bhonsle Treaty of Mandsaur; complete abolition of the Peshwa's office; Baji Rao II exiled to Bithoor; Maratha Confederacy dissolved
First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–1846 CE) Governor-General: Lord Hardinge I; Duleep Singh & Lal Singh vs. British Treaty of Lahore (1846); Jalandhar Doab ceded to the British; Kashmir sold to Gulab Singh for 1 crore rupees
Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–1849 CE) Governor-General: Lord Dalhousie; Sikh Army vs. Lord Gough Battle of Gujrat / Battle of Guns (1849); complete British annexation of Punjab on 29 March 1849; Koh-i-Noor sent to the Queen

Major States Accepting the Subsidiary Alliance and Key Terms
Analytical Overview of Lord Wellesley's (1798–1805) Imperial Expansion Policy
State & Year of Treaty Signatory Indian Ruler Key Terms & Geopolitical Outcomes
Hyderabad (1798 & 1800 CE) Nizam Ali Khan (Asaf Jah II) First state to accept the alliance; disbanded French troops; stationed a British Resident; ceded Bellary and Cuddapah districts for maintenance costs

Maratha Peshwa (December 1802 CE) Peshwa Baji Rao II (Treaty of Bassein) 6,000 British troops stationed at Pune; territory worth 26 lakh rupees ceded for upkeep; marked the beginning of the Maratha Confederacy's disintegration
Bhonsle & Scindia (1803 CE) Raghuji Bhonsle (Deogaon) & Daulat Rao Scindia (Surji-Anjangaon) Ceded Cuttack, Ganga-Yamuna Doab, and Bundelkhand to the British; stationing a British Resident at their courts made mandatory

Doctrine of Lapse
Dalhousie's Doctrine of Lapse — Annexed States, Rulers, and Grounds
Chronology of the Annexation of Princely States into British India by Prohibiting Adoption
State & Year of Annexation Contemporary Ruler & Status Grounds of Annexation & Policy Framework
Satara (1848 CE) Appa Sahib (Adopted Son: Anandrao) First victim of the Doctrine of Lapse; claim of the adopted son declared invalid upon the ruler's death without a natural heir
Jaitpur & Sambalpur (1849 CE) Chhatrasal (Jaitpur, Bundelkhand) & Narayan Singh (Sambalpur, Odisha) Doctrine of Lapse; both rulers died without a natural heir; directly annexed on grounds of lacking a legitimate successor
Baghat (1850 CE) Rana Vijay Singh (Hill State of Himachal Pradesh) Doctrine of Lapse; died without a natural heir; (subsequently restored in 1856 by Lord Canning on orders from the Court of Directors)
Udaipur (1852 CE) Shivraj Singh (Central Provinces / Chhattisgarh) Doctrine of Lapse; died without a natural heir; (later in 1854, the Court of Directors annulled the annexation and restored it)
Jhansi (1853 CE) Gangadhar Rao (Rani Lakshmibai; Adopted Son: Damodar) Doctrine of Lapse; following Gangadhar Rao's death, refused to recognize Rani Lakshmibai's adopted son Damodar Rao as heir
Nagpur (1854 CE) Raghuji Bhonsle III (Adopted Son: Yashwantrao) Doctrine of Lapse; died without a natural heir; royal palace jewels, elephants, and horses were publicly auctioned in Calcutta
Awadh (February 1856 CE) Nawab Wajid Ali Shah (Capital: Lucknow) Not Doctrine of Lapse, but 'Allegation of Misgovernance' (James Outram Report); Nawab exiled; became a major epicenter of the 1857 Revolt
