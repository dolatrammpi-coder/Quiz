Historic campaigns across North, West, East, and South India from 1561 to 1601 CE
Military Campaign & Year Region & Opponent Strategic Outcome & Historical Impact
Malwa Campaign (1561–62 CE) Malwa (Sarangpur) Opponent: Baz Bahadur Adham Khan & Pir Muhammad; Baz Bahadur defeated; Rani Roopmati consumed poison; Malwa annexed into the Mughal Empire
Gondwana Campaign (1564 CE) Garha-Katanga (Madhya Pradesh) Opponent: Rani Durgavati & Bir Narayan Led by Asaf Khan; Rani Durgavati fought heroically and sacrificed her life; immense gold and elephants captured

Bengal-Bihar Campaign (1574–76 CE) Patna, Gaur, Tukaroi Opponent: Daud Khan Karrani Munim Khan & Akbar; Afghans defeated in Battles of Tukaroi and Rajmahal; complete annexation of Eastern India into the Mughal Empire
Battle of Haldighati (18 June 1576) Near Gogunda (Mewar) Opponent: Maharana Pratap Man Singh & Asaf Khan; indomitable valor of Pratap; indecisive strategic outcome; Pratap continued struggle from Chavand
Conquest of Kabul & Kashmir (1581 & 1586 CE) Afghanistan & Kashmir Valley Opponent: Mirza Hakim & Yusuf Chak Half-brother suppressed in Kabul; Kashmir conquered by Qasim Khan and Bhagwant Das and made a Mughal Subah
Asirgarh Campaign (1601 CE) Khandesh (Gateway to the Deccan) Opponent: Miran Bahadur Last military campaign of Akbar's life; 'the fort was opened with keys of gold' via bribery; paved the way to the Deccan

Administrative Framework
Akbar's Central Administrative System and Major Departments/Officials
Centralized Mughal administrative machinery based on checks and balances
Central Post / Department Major Functions & Powers Associated Officials & Nature
Wakil (Wakil-i-Mutlaq) Highest administrative post after the Emperor; initially unlimited powers, later rendered powerless Bairam Khan & Munim Khan; Akbar stripped its financial powers and transferred them to the Wazir
Diwan / Wazir (Diwan-i-Kul) Supreme finance and revenue head of the empire; control over all income-expenditure, Jagirs, and Khalisa Muzaffar Khan Turbati, Raja Todar Mal, Khwaja Shah Mansur; functioned directly under the Emperor
Mir Bakshi (Military Department) Military administration, recruitment of Mansabdars, Dagh-Huliya of horses, issuing salary certificates (Sarkhat) Military head, though not necessarily the battlefield commander; collected intelligence reports from Barids
Mir Saman / Khan-i-Saman Supreme manager of the imperial household, inner palace, harem, and royal workshops (36 Karkhanas) Highly trusted officer of the Emperor; maintained royal stores, provisions, robes, and weaponry
Sadr-us-Sudur (Religious Department) Head of religious affairs, Islamic education, Waqf properties, and tax-free land grants (Madad-i-Maash / Suyurghal) Often held the responsibility of Qazi-ul-Quzat (Chief Justice) as well
Muhtasib & Mir-i-Adl Muhtasib: guardian of public conduct and morality; Mir-i-Adl: executor of the Qazi's judicial verdicts Inspection of weights and measures in towns, curb on gambling and liquor, and impartial judicial enforcement

Military System
Mansabdari System — Zat, Sawar, Ranks, and Key Features
Mughal military and civil administrative cadre based on the Mongol decimal system
Component / Dimension Administrative Structure & Rules Historical Impact & Objective
Origin & Structure (Institutionalized in 1577 CE) Inspired by Genghis Khan's decimal military system; assigning each officer a specific 'Mansab' (rank/status) Unified framework for civil and military officers; end of hereditary posts; personal loyalty to the Emperor
Zat Rank (Personal Prestige) Numerical indicator determining an officer's personal status, administrative precedence, and cash/jagir salary Criterion for seating in court, prestige, and pay; Mansabs ranged from 10 to 10,000
Sawar Rank (Military Obligation) Indicator of the actual number of cavalrymen and horses a Mansabdar was required to maintain Sawar rank could never exceed Zat rank; ensured rapid military mobilization during wartime
3 Categories of Mansabdars (Zat-Sawar Ratio) First Class: Zat = Sawar; Second Class: Sawar more than half of Zat; Third Class: Sawar less than half of Zat Precise scientific determination of salaries and allowances; promotion based on a Mansabdar's efficiency
Dagh, Chehra & Jagir (Corruption Control) Double royal branding of horses (Dagh) and descriptive roll of soldiers (Chehra); salary in cash (Naqdi) or Jagir Complete check on fake musters and inferior horses; regular transfer of Jagirs to prevent feudal entrenchment

Land Revenue System
Akbar's Land Revenue System — Dahsala, Land Classification, and Measurement
Scientific and permanent revenue system developed by Raja Todar Mal (1580 CE)
Revenue Element / Component Process, Nature & Rules Associated Officials & Economic Impact
Ain-i-Dahsala (1580 CE) Revenue fixed by calculating average produce and average prices of the past 10 years (1571–1580); tax set at 1/3rd of produce Raja Todar Mal & Khwaja Shah Mansur; eliminated uncertainty in tax assessment and hassle of annual surveys
Land Measurement Reforms (Ilahi Gaz & Jarib) 41-digit (33.5-inch) Ilahi Gaz replaced Sikandari Gaz; bamboo Jarib with iron rings replaced hemp rope Amins & Qanungos; ended measurement errors caused by moisture and stretching; accurate Bigha area (3600 sq. yards)
Polaj & Parauti Land (Regular Cultivation) Polaj: cultivated regularly every year without break; Parauti: left fallow for 1–2 years to regain fertility Amalguzar; highest revenue yield; 1/3rd tax in cash or kind based on average of good, middling, and bad produce
Chachar & Banjar Land (Low-Cultivation Land) Chachar: left uncultivated for 3–4 years; Banjar: uncultivated for 5 or more years; concessional initial rates Taqavi loans to peasants to reclaim barren land; full rate applied in the 4th/5th year to expand agriculture
Karori System (1573 CE) Dividing the empire into circles yielding 1 crore Dam (2.5 lakh rupees) in annual land revenue Karori officers; collection of actual agricultural production data, laying the foundation for Dahsala in 1580

Religious Policy
Akbar's Religious Policy — Key Events, Institutions, and Policy Shifts
Gradual ideological evolution from orthodoxy to universal tolerance (Sulh-i-Kul)
Religious Institution / Policy & Year Objective & Nature Contemporary Impact & Historical Significance
Abolition of Pilgrimage Tax & Jizya (1563 & 1564 CE) Complete abolition of centuries-old discriminatory religious taxes imposed on non-Muslim Hindu subjects Rise of loyalty and trust toward the Emperor among Hindu subjects; foundation of a national empire
Establishment of Ibadat Khana (1575 CE, Fatehpur Sikri) Hall of worship initially for religious debates among Sunni Ulema only; opened to all religions in 1578 Exposed the hypocrisy and narrow sectarian disputes of the Ulema; Akbar's disillusionment with orthodox Islam
Proclamation of Mahzarnama (September 1579 CE) Drafted by Shaikh Mubarak; declared Akbar 'Imam-i-Adil' (final arbiter in religious matters) Ended the Ulema's monopoly over religious politics; prioritized state interest over narrow interpretations of Shariat
Principle of Sulh-i-Kul (Universal Peace) Complete tolerance, respect, and non-discriminatory harmony toward all religions; philosophical basis by Abul Fazl Core guiding principle of Mughal polity; successful integration of diverse cultures, castes, and sects
Tauhid-i-Ilahi / Din-i-Ilahi (1582 CE) Synthesis of noble ethical elements of all religions; not a new religion or prophet, but a moral code of conduct Initiation on Sundays; accepted by only 18 prominent courtiers; Birbal the sole Hindu member; no forced conversions
