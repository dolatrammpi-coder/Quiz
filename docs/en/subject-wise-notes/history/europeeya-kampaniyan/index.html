Major European Powers/Companies — Establishment, Arrival, and Nature of Trade
Major European trading organizations established in India from the 15th to the 17th century
European Power & Organization Arrival / Establishment & First Centre Key Activities & Nature of Trade
Portuguese State (Estado da Índia) Arrival: 1498 CE First Centre: Cochin (1503 CE) Royal state-controlled trade; Cartaz-Armada system; monopoly over spice trade; supremacy in the Indian Ocean
Dutch East India Company (VOC - Netherlands) Company Founded: 1602 CE First Centre: Masulipatnam (1605 CE) Joint-stock company; spice trade from Indonesia and cotton textile exports from India; issued gold Pagoda coins
English East India Company (The Governor and Company...) Company Founded: 31 December 1600 CE First Centre: Surat (1613 CE) Company of private merchant shareholders; royal farmans of 1617 and 1717; transition from trade to political dominance and territorial conquest
Danish East India Company (Denmark) Company Founded: 1616 CE First Centre: Tranquebar (1620 CE) Limited commercial activities; Serampore (Bengal) as main centre; sold all settlements to the British in 1845 CE
French East India Company (Compagnie des Indes Orientales) Company Founded: 1664 CE First Centre: Surat (1668 CE) Fully state-financed government company; Pondicherry as main administrative centre; imperial expansion ambitions under Dupleix

Trade Strategy
Commercial Strategy and Political Expansion of Major European Powers in India
Historical journey from commercial monopoly to territorial sovereignty
European Power Initial Commercial Strategy Political Expansion & Colonial Direction
Portuguese Direct monopoly over spice trade; ban on non-Portuguese ships in the Indian Ocean; Cartaz taxation system Capture of coastal forts and ports; religious intolerance and conversion; remained confined to a maritime empire rather than territorial expansion
Dutch (Netherlands) Focus on Indonesia for spices; using India as a supply base for cotton textiles; emphasis on low cost and high dividends Purely commercial outlook; made no attempt at political conquest over large Indian territories; expelled after the Battle of Bedara in 1759
English (British) Trade in cotton textiles, indigo, and saltpetre from Surat and coastal centres; obtaining royal trade farmans from Mughal emperors Fortification of trading centres; military intervention in internal disputes of Indian princely states; became sovereign rulers after Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764)
French Colbert's state-owned company; luxury and textile trade from Pondicherry and Chandernagore; cordial relations with local rulers Attempt at political dominance by Governor Dupleix by hiring out troops in Indian succession disputes; defeated in the Carnatic Wars

The Three Carnatic Wars — Years, Belligerents, Causes, and Concluding Treaties
Struggle for supreme colonial dominance in South India from 1746 to 1763 CE
Carnatic War & Years Belligerents & Key Commanders Main Causes, Events & Concluding Treaty
First Carnatic War (1746–1748 CE) English (Barnett, Peyton) vs. French (Dupleix, La Bourdonnais) and Nawab Anwar-ud-din Cause: War of the Austrian Succession in Europe; Battle of San Thome in 1746 (Adyar River); La Bourdonnais captured Madras; Conclusion: Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748 (Madras returned to the English)
Second Carnatic War (1749–1754 CE) English (Clive, Muhammad Ali, Nasir Jung) vs. French (Dupleix, Chanda Sahib, Muzaffar Jung) Cause: Succession disputes in Hyderabad and Carnatic; Battle of Ambur in 1749; Clive's Siege of Arcot in 1751; Conclusion: Treaty of Pondicherry in 1754 (signed by Godeheu; Dupleix recalled to France)
Third Carnatic War (1758–1763 CE) British Army (Sir Eyre Coote) vs. French Army (Count de Lally and Bussy) Cause: Seven Years' War in Europe (1756–63); decisive Battle of Wandiwash on 22 January 1760 (Lally defeated); Conclusion: Treaty of Paris in 1763 (French military expansion completely ended)
