Islamic orthodoxy, strict adherence to Sharia, and policy shifts
Religious Decision / Policy & Year Target Area / Group & Nature Historical Impact & Evidence
Ban on Kalima on Coins & Nauroz (1659 CE) Currency and court festivities; Persian festival of Nauroz and Kalima inscription abolished Implementation of strict Islamic theological rules; simplification of court traditions

Rebellion & Resistance
Major Rebellions/Conflicts, Leaders, and Outcomes During Aurangzeb's Reign
Chronological overview of Jat, Satnami, Sikh, Bundela, and Afghan uprisings
Rebellion / Conflict & Period Key Leaders & Rebel Side Cause of Rebellion & Suppression / Outcome
Jat Rebellion (1669–1688 CE) Gokula, Rajaram, and Churaman Jat (Mathura, Bharatpur, and Agra) Atrocities of Abdun Nabi and land revenue exploitation; Gokula martyred; Rajaram burned Akbar's bones; Churaman laid the foundation of the independent Bharatpur state
Satnami Rebellion (1672 CE) Satnami ascetics (Mundiya sect) (Narnaul and Mewat, Haryana) Mistreatment of a Satnami peasant by a Mughal soldier; religious and agrarian discontent; suppressed by Aurangzeb through heavy artillery massacre
Sikh Struggle (1675 & 1699–1705 CE) Guru Tegh Bahadur and Guru Gobind Singh (Punjab and Anandpur Sahib) Guru Tegh Bahadur beheaded in 1675 for opposing the persecution of Kashmiri Pandits; Khalsa Panth founded in 1699; Battle of Chamkaur
Bundela Rebellion (1671–1707 CE) Raja Chhatrasal Bundela (Bundelkhand) Mughal religious intolerance and exploitation; Chhatrasal drew inspiration from Shivaji; defeated the Mughals and established the independent Panna state
Afghan Rebellions (1667 & 1672 CE) Bhagu (Yusufzai) and Akmal Khan (Afridi) (North-West Frontier) Desire for independence; several Mughal commanders (Shujaat Khan) killed; Aurangzeb himself had to go to Hasan Abdal to suppress them through diplomacy

Deccan Policy
Aurangzeb's Deccan Policy — Major Campaigns, States, and Outcomes
The 25-year ruinous military entanglement in South India from 1681 to 1707 CE
Deccan Campaign & Period Target State & Key Opponent Military Action & Historical Outcome
Departure for the Deccan (1681 CE) Rebel Prince Akbar and the Maratha State (Maharashtra) Aurangzeb marched to the Deccan pursuing Prince Akbar; spent the last 25 years of his life there; never returned to Delhi
Conquest of Bijapur (1685–1686 CE) Adil Shahi Sultanate Ruler: Sikandar Adil Shah 15-month strict siege; forced surrender by cutting off supplies; on 12 September 1686, Bijapur became a subah of the Mughal Empire
Conquest of Golconda (1687 CE) Qutb Shahi Sultanate Ruler: Abul Hasan (Tana Shah) 8-month siege; gates opened by bribing Abdullah Khan Panni; annexed on 21 September 1687; immense treasury captured
Execution of Sambhaji (1689 CE) Maratha Chhatrapati Place: Sangameshwar and Tulapur Muqarrab Khan captured him at Sangameshwar; upon refusing to embrace Islam, brutally executed at Tulapur on 11 March 1689
Maratha War of Independence (1689–1707 CE) Chhatrapati Rajaram and Maharani Tarabai (Jinji and Satara) Maratha people's war and guerrilla strikes; destruction of Mughal treasury and prestige; the Deccan proved fatal like Napoleon's Spanish ulcer

Crisis of the Empire
Political, Military, Economic, and Administrative Challenges in the Transition of the Mughal Empire
Structural factors behind imperial decline in the late 17th and early 18th centuries
Dimension / Area of Challenge Structural Crisis & Causes Impact on the Decline of the Mughal Empire
Jagirdari Crisis (Be-jagiri situation) Excessive increase in the number of mansabdars (especially Deccani nobles); shortage of fertile jagirs (paibaqi) According to Satish Chandra, factionalism among mansabdars; arrears in soldiers' pay caused complete erosion of military loyalty
Agrarian Discontent & Revolts (Zamindari Crisis) According to Irfan Habib, excessive land revenue demand (50% of produce); severe exploitation by intermediaries and zamindars Violent peasant uprisings in the form of Jats, Satnamis, Sikhs, and Marathas; devastation of the rural economy
Deccan Strategic Quagmire (Imperial Over-stretch) Emperor's absence from the capital for 25 years; administrative laxity in North India due to slow communications Treasury completely depleted; commanders and soldiers exhausted and demoralized by war; central control virtually collapsed
Religious & Social Alienation (Rajput Estrangement) Jizya, temple demolition, and unwarranted interference in Marwar-Mewar succession; orthodox Sharia policy Loyal support of the 'Rajputs', the strongest military pillar of the Mughal Empire, ended forever
Absence of Law of Succession (Court Factionalism) Throne decided by the sword; flood of incompetent, pleasure-seeking, and short-lived rulers after Aurangzeb's death Rise of court factions like the Sayyid Brothers (Kingmakers); Nadir Shah's invasion in 1739 sealed the empire's fate
