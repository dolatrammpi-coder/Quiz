Exchange Rate Part 2

A Comprehensive Analysis of Historical Rupee Devaluations (1949, 1966, 1991), Devaluation vs. Depreciation, Dollar Exchange Rate Movements, and Macroeconomic Impacts

🔍

Key Facts & Contributions
Exchange Rate Part 2
Exchange Rate ChangeKey Cause / FeaturePotential Impact
First Devaluation of 1949Carried out in alignment with the 30.5% devaluation of the British Pound Sterling; driven by trade imbalances from Partition and protecting the Sterling Area.The rupee moved from ₹3.30 to ₹4.76 against the US dollar; reduced non-sterling imports and brought immediate improvement in exports to the dollar area.
Second Devaluation of 1966Triggered by the 1962 and 1965 wars, the severe 1965–66 drought, depleted forex reserves, and conditional assistance from the World Bank/IMF (Bell Mission).A steep 36.5% devaluation of the rupee (from ₹4.76 to ₹7.50 per dollar); caused a sharp rise in the import bill and political controversy, though improving long-term export competitiveness.
Third Devaluation of 1991Driven by the 1990 Gulf War, surging oil prices, forex reserves falling to $1.1B, and the precondition for an emergency IMF loan.Cumulative devaluation of ~18.5% in two steps (1 and 3 July); halted capital flight, initiated the LERMS dual-rate system, and paved the way for a market float in 1993.
DevaluationA deliberate reduction in the external value of the domestic currency by administrative order of the government or central bank under a fixed or pegged exchange rate regime.Makes exports cheaper and imports costlier; eases pressure on the central bank's forex reserves; generally a politically unpopular move.
DepreciationAn automatic fall in the value of the domestic currency driven by market forces of demand and supply under a flexible / floating exchange rate system.Ensures daily market equilibrium and automatic absorption of external shocks (such as oil spikes or Fed rate hikes); increases servicing costs of unhedged foreign debt.
Strengthening of the US DollarDriven by aggressive interest rate hikes by the US Federal Reserve, global geopolitical tensions, and surging safe-haven demand.Triggers FPI capital outflows from emerging markets including India; puts heavy depreciation pressure on domestic currencies and makes dollar-denominated imports costlier.
J-Curve EffectDominance of the Price Effect over the Volume Effect in the short run immediately following a devaluation or depreciation.The trade deficit widens further during the initial 6 to 12 months, before achieving durable improvement in the medium run via export expansion and import contraction.
Fulfilment of the Marshall-Lerner ConditionThe sum of price elasticities of demand for exports and imports exceeds unity (|e_x + e_m| > 1).Devaluation or depreciation successfully improves the country's trade balance and narrows the Current Account Deficit (CAD).

One-Liner Questions

What is 'Currency Devaluation' in economics?
A deliberate reduction in the value of the domestic currency relative to a foreign reference currency by an official policy decision of the government or central bank under a fixed or pegged exchange rate system.

How many times has the Indian rupee been formally devalued in the economic history of independent India?
On three major historical occasions: 1. September 1949, 2. June 1966, and 3. July 1991.
Tap to view answer
In what historical context was independent India's first rupee devaluation carried out in September 1949?
In alignment with Britain's decision to devalue the Pound Sterling by 30.5% against the US dollar.
Tap to view answer
Who was the Union Finance Minister of India at the time of the 1949 devaluation?
Dr. John Matthai.
Tap to view answer
Against which currency was the rupee devalued in 1949, and against which did it remain unchanged?
The rupee was devalued by 30.5% against the US dollar (falling from $1 = ₹3.30 to ₹4.76), while its exchange rate with the British Pound (1 shilling 6 pence per rupee) remained unchanged.
Tap to view answer
Why was it imperative for the Government of India to devalue the rupee alongside the Pound Sterling in 1949?
Because India was an integral member of the 'Sterling Area', and most of India's foreign trade was conducted with Britain and Commonwealth countries.
Tap to view answer
What unexpected impact did the 1949 devaluation have on India–Pakistan trade?
Pakistan refused to devalue its currency, making the Pakistani rupee costlier than the Indian rupee and bringing bilateral trade in jute and cotton between the two countries to a standstill.
Tap to view answer
Who were the Prime Minister and Finance Minister during India's second major devaluation in June 1966?
Prime Minister Indira Gandhi and Finance Minister Sachindra Chaudhuri.
Tap to view answer
By what percentage was the Indian rupee steeply devalued on 6 June 1966?
An official devaluation of 36.5% (the rupee fell directly from ₹4.76 to ₹7.50 per US dollar).
Tap to view answer
What was the impact of the 1966 devaluation against the British Pound?
The rupee was devalued against the pound as well; the price of 1 pound rose from ₹13.33 to ₹21.00.
Tap to view answer
Which internal crises forced the Government of India to undertake such a severe devaluation in 1966?
The 1962 India–China War, the 1965 India–Pakistan War, the worst drought of the century in 1965–66, and the depletion of foreign exchange reserves.
Tap to view answer
Which international institutional pressure played a decisive role behind the 1966 devaluation?
The World Bank and the International Monetary Fund (IMF); specifically, the World Bank's 'Bernard Bell Mission' report, which made devaluation a mandatory condition for foreign aid.
Tap to view answer
What complementary policy changes were made to the import–export regime alongside the 1966 devaluation?
Cash subsidies provided for export promotion were abolished, and customs tariffs were rationalized.
Tap to view answer
Why was the 1966 devaluation considered a short-term economic and political failure?
Because another severe drought struck in 1966–67, preventing agricultural exports from rising, while the import bill for essentials (foodgrains, fertilizers) surged and triggered high inflation.
Tap to view answer
When and in how many stages was the rupee devalued during the historic 1991 Balance of Payments crisis?
In July 1991 in two stages: the first step on 1 July 1991 (~9%) and the second step on 3 July 1991 (~11%).
Tap to view answer
What was the total cumulative devaluation of the rupee in the two-step devaluation of July 1991?
A cumulative devaluation of approximately 18.5% to 19% against major foreign currencies (USD, GBP, DEM, JPY).
Tap to view answer
Who were the Prime Minister, Finance Minister, and RBI Governor of India during the 1991 devaluation?
Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao, Finance Minister Dr. Manmohan Singh, and RBI Governor S. Venkitaramanan.
Tap to view answer
Why was the July 1991 devaluation split across two separate days (1 and 3 July) instead of a single shock?
To gauge the immediate reaction of the forex market, thwart speculation, and prevent market panic.
Tap to view answer
What was the official value of 1 US dollar just before the 1991 devaluation, and what did it become afterward?
Before devaluation, 1 USD was approximately ₹21.20; after 3 July 1991, it rose to roughly ₹25.95 per dollar.
Tap to view answer
What was the immediate macroeconomic objective of the 1991 devaluation?
To immediately halt rapid capital flight, bring remittances back from hawala channels into the official banking system, and secure an emergency loan facility from the IMF.
Tap to view answer
Which interim arrangement was introduced in the March 1992 Budget for exchange rate reform?
The Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System (LERMS), known as the 'Dual Exchange Rate System'.
Tap to view answer
What was the rule for converting foreign exchange earnings under the LERMS dual exchange rate system?
A 40:60 ratio: 40% of forex earnings had to be surrendered to the government at the official rate for essential imports (oil, fertilizers), while 60% could be converted at the market rate.
Tap to view answer
When did India permanently end the system of official administrative devaluation and adopt a market-based exchange rate?
In the Union Budget of 1 March 1993, when the Unified Market-Determined Exchange Rate System was introduced.
Tap to view answer
Since 1993, what is any fall in the value of the Indian rupee officially termed?
Currency 'Depreciation', not Devaluation, because the rate is now determined by market demand and supply.
Tap to view answer
What dramatic impact did the 1991 devaluation and subsequent reforms have on India's foreign exchange reserves?
Forex reserves jumped from just $1.1 billion in June 1991 to $15 billion by March 1994, and crossed $100 billion by 2004.
Tap to view answer
What is the most fundamental institutional difference between 'Devaluation' and 'Depreciation'?
Devaluation is carried out by administrative order of the government or central bank, whereas depreciation occurs automatically through the forces of demand and supply in the forex market.
Tap to view answer
To which types of exchange rate regimes do Devaluation and Depreciation respectively belong?
Devaluation belongs to a Fixed/Pegged exchange rate system, whereas Depreciation belongs to a Flexible/Floating exchange rate system.
Tap to view answer
How do Devaluation and Depreciation differ in terms of frequency of occurrence?
Devaluation is a discrete, sudden, and rare policy event, whereas depreciation is a continuous, dynamic fluctuation occurring every second in the market.
Tap to view answer
What is the difference between 'Revaluation' and 'Appreciation'?
Revaluation is an official upward revision of a currency's value by the government under a fixed rate system, whereas Appreciation is an automatic strengthening of a currency by market forces under a floating system.
Tap to view answer
What difference is observed between Devaluation and Depreciation regarding political accountability?
Direct political responsibility for devaluation falls on the government and is viewed as a policy failure, whereas depreciation is seen as the outcome of global market conditions and impersonal economic forces.
Tap to view answer
Does Currency Depreciation require any formal legal order or gazette notification from the central bank?
No; it is reflected automatically through underlying transactions in the foreign exchange market.
Tap to view answer
Can a country's central bank intervene under a floating system to slow down the pace of depreciation?
Yes, by selling dollars from its forex reserves (under a Managed Float), though it does not officially fix the rate.
Tap to view answer
What differing impacts do Devaluation and Depreciation have on international financial credibility?
Devaluation sends a stark signal of sovereign debt crisis and international panic, whereas gradual depreciation is viewed as a normal market adjustment of the economy.
Tap to view answer
Under which system is the risk of 'Speculative Attacks' more destructive?
Under a Fixed Exchange Rate system (prior to devaluation), because speculators know the central bank has limited reserves and can earn one-way profits.
Tap to view answer
How is Depreciation superior to Devaluation in terms of internal Policy Autonomy?
A floating rate (depreciation) allows the central bank to set interest rates independently based on domestic priorities (inflation, growth), whereas under a fixed rate, interest rates must be raised forcibly to defend the currency.
Tap to view answer
How do Devaluation and Depreciation differ in terms of Cost of Defense?
Defending a fixed rate can completely exhaust the central bank's forex reserves, whereas under a floating rate, the currency adjusts automatically to a new equilibrium, preserving reserves.
Tap to view answer
When news reports state that 'the rupee fell 20 paise against the dollar to close at ₹83.40', which concept is this an example of?
Daily market Depreciation of the rupee.
Tap to view answer
When the government announced in 1966 that '1 dollar will now be worth ₹7.50 instead of ₹4.76', what was that an example of?
Official administrative Devaluation.
Tap to view answer
Are the ultimate trade effects (cheaper exports, costlier imports) of both Devaluation and Depreciation identical?
Yes; the economic outcomes are identical—only the underlying mechanism, timeframe, and regulatory environment differ.
Tap to view answer
Under which regime is the need for commercial Hedging felt most acutely?
Under a Floating Rate regime (depreciation risk), because daily fluctuations make profit margins unpredictable for importers and exporters.
Tap to view answer
Why is the US Dollar (USD) regarded as the world's primary Global Reserve Currency in the forex market?
Because over ~85% of global trade invoicing and ~58% of global central banks' foreign exchange reserves are held in US dollars.
Tap to view answer
What is the US Dollar Index (DXY)?
A global index measuring the value of the US dollar relative to a basket of six major developed currencies (Euro, Japanese Yen, British Pound, Canadian Dollar, Swedish Krona, and Swiss Franc).
Tap to view answer
Which currency holds the largest weight in the US Dollar Index (DXY)?
The Euro (EUR), accounting for roughly 57.6% of the total basket.
Tap to view answer
When the US Dollar Index (DXY) rises globally from 100 to 106, what natural pressure builds on the Indian rupee?
Global strengthening of the US dollar puts heavy automatic depreciation pressure on the Indian rupee against the dollar.
Tap to view answer
What is the direct impact of aggressive Fed Rate Hikes by the US Federal Reserve on the Indian rupee?
The Interest Rate Differential between India and the US narrows, prompting Foreign Portfolio Investors (FPIs) to pull dollars out of India into US bonds, weakening the rupee.
Tap to view answer
How does a sharp spike in international Crude Oil prices weaken the Indian rupee?
Because India imports 85% of its oil needs; costlier oil forces Indian importers to demand huge volumes of dollars, making the dollar costlier and weakening the rupee.
Tap to view answer
How does a widening Current Account Deficit (CAD) in India affect the dollar–rupee exchange rate?
Outflows of foreign exchange exceed inflows, increasing net demand for dollars and causing the rupee to depreciate.
Tap to view answer
What happens when Foreign Institutional/Portfolio Investors (FIIs/FPIs) engage in heavy net selling in Indian stock markets?
They sell their Indian equities, convert rupees into dollars, and remit them abroad, creating an acute dollar shortage in the market and pulling the rupee down.
Tap to view answer
How does the dollar behave during 'Risk-Off Sentiment' (Flight to Safety) in global financial markets?
During geopolitical tensions or wars, global investors pull capital from emerging markets and seek refuge in the safest assets (US Treasury bonds and the dollar), causing the dollar to strengthen sharply.
Tap to view answer
When the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) intervenes in the forex market to stem the rupee's slide, what does it sell?
The RBI sells US dollars from its foreign exchange reserves in the open market and absorbs rupee liquidity from the system.
Tap to view answer
What is the immediate effect of the RBI selling dollars on the country's foreign exchange reserves?
Foreign exchange reserves decline (for instance, after the 2022 Ukraine war, reserves fell from $642 billion to $524 billion).
Tap to view answer
Why does 'Unhedged ECB Exposure' (External Commercial Borrowings) by Indian companies become dangerous when the dollar strengthens?
Because companies earn revenues in rupees but must repay debt in dollars; a stronger dollar abruptly inflates their debt servicing costs and heightens bankruptcy risk.
Tap to view answer
How does a stronger dollar affect Indian students studying abroad and Indian tourists?
Foreign tuition fees, living expenses, and international air travel become substantially costlier in rupee terms.
Tap to view answer
What impact does a stronger dollar have on Remittances sent to India by Non-Resident Indians (NRIs)?
Migrants receive more rupees for every dollar sent, incentivizing them to remit more funds to India and place fixed deposits (FCNR/NRE).
Tap to view answer
How steeply did the rupee fall against the dollar during the 2013 'Taper Tantrum' crisis?
Between May 2013 and August 2013, the rupee plunged from roughly ₹54 per dollar to a then-record low of ₹68.80 per dollar.
Tap to view answer
Which successful scheme did RBI Governor Dr. Raghuram Rajan launch to rescue the rupee from the 2013 Taper Tantrum crisis?
The FCNR(B) special concessional dollar swap window, through which Indian banks raised $34 billion in fresh foreign exchange from overseas markets.
Tap to view answer
What was the trajectory of the Indian rupee against the US dollar following the Russia–Ukraine War (2022)?
With crude oil crossing $130 per barrel and the US Fed hiking interest rates by 500 basis points, the rupee fell from ₹74 to the ₹82–₹83 per dollar range.
Tap to view answer
Does a fall in the rupee against the dollar always signify internal weakness of the Indian economy?
No; it is often the result of global US dollar strength (a surging DXY); in fact, the rupee has frequently appreciated against other major global currencies (Euro, Yen, Pound) at the same time.
Tap to view answer
What is the significance of 'Orderly Depreciation' in the RBI's exchange rate policy?
Allowing the rupee to adjust gradually in line with market realities, while preventing speculators from triggering sharp single-day swings or market panic.
Tap to view answer
What intervention does the RBI undertake in the Non-Deliverable Forward (NDF) market to ensure stability in the dollar–rupee exchange rate?
Permitting Indian banks located in GIFT City (GIFT IFSC) to participate in the offshore NDF market so as to curb overseas speculation.
Tap to view answer
What is the primary and stated objective behind a country's official devaluation of its currency?
To eliminate a severe Balance of Payments (BoP) deficit and halt the rapid depletion of official foreign exchange reserves.
Tap to view answer
Through which economic mechanism does devaluation boost exports?
By lowering the foreign-currency price of domestic goods for overseas buyers, making them price-competitive in international markets.
Tap to view answer
How does devaluation achieve 'Import Compression'?
By raising the domestic-currency price of foreign goods, making them excessively expensive at home and encouraging consumers to shift to domestic substitutes.
Tap to view answer
How does devaluation curb the informal or parallel foreign exchange market (hawala market)?
By bringing the official exchange rate closer to the realistic market rate instead of keeping it artificially strong, thereby wiping out the premium between official and hawala rates.
Tap to view answer
How does devaluation encourage 'Capital Repatriation' into a country?
By reassuring foreign investors and expatriates that a realistic currency correction has occurred and that the risk of sudden further devaluation is gone.
Tap to view answer
What does the 'J-Curve Effect' illustrate regarding devaluation and the trade balance?
Immediately after devaluation, the trade deficit worsens in the short run (the downward dip of the J), but in the medium to long run, as volumes adjust, the trade balance improves strongly (the upward curve of the J).
Tap to view answer
Why does the trade deficit worsen instead of improving in the short run under the J-Curve Effect?
Due to the 'Price Effect': payments for pre-existing import contracts must immediately be made at the higher exchange rate, while export volumes cannot expand instantaneously.
Tap to view answer
Why does the trade balance improve in the second phase of the J-Curve Effect?
Due to the dominance of the 'Volume Effect', as foreign buyers place new orders for cheaper domestic goods and domestic buyers cut back on costlier imports.
Tap to view answer
What are the main causes of this adjustment Time Lag between the short run and the long run?
Rigid consumer habits, existing commercial contracts, the time required to expand factory production capacity, and the time needed to explore new international markets.
Tap to view answer
What does the 'Marshall-Lerner Condition' propound?
Currency devaluation will improve the trade deficit only if the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports exceeds 1 (|e_x + e_m| > 1).
Tap to view answer
If the Marshall-Lerner Condition is not satisfied in a country (|e_x + e_m| < 1), what will be the outcome of devaluation on the trade deficit?
Instead of shrinking, the trade deficit will widen further (Perverse Effect).
Tap to view answer
Why does the Marshall-Lerner Condition often fail in the short run in developing countries?
Because their essential imports (crude oil, life-saving medicines, heavy machinery) are highly inelastic, meaning demand cannot be reduced even when prices rise.
Tap to view answer
How does Sidney Alexander's 'Absorption Approach' analyse the impact of devaluation?
Trade Balance = Total National Income (Y) - Total Domestic Absorption (A = C + I + G); devaluation can improve the trade balance only if it raises total domestic output or reduces domestic consumption/absorption.
Tap to view answer
If an economy is already operating at Full Employment, what will be the outcome of devaluation?
Output cannot expand immediately; additional export demand will merely fuel severe domestic inflation, reducing the real benefit of devaluation to zero.
Tap to view answer
What is the most dangerous and negative side effect of devaluation?
'Cost-Push / Imported Inflation', as costlier imported raw materials, energy, and equipment drive up overall domestic production costs.
Tap to view answer
What adverse impact does devaluation have on the government budget and fiscal deficit?
The government's external debt servicing obligations (interest and principal repayments) rise in rupee terms, and the subsidy bill on imported fertilizers and petroleum balloons.
Tap to view answer
What is the concept of 'Contractionary Devaluation' in economics, propounded by Paul Krugman and Lance Taylor?
A situation where devaluation, instead of expanding the economy, contracts (reduces) real national income, consumption, and the economic growth rate.
Tap to view answer
Why does Contractionary Devaluation occur in developing countries?
Because inflation erodes real wages and depresses domestic demand, corporate balance sheets suffer from heavier foreign debt burdens, and inelastic import bills transfer domestic purchasing power abroad.
Tap to view answer
What impact does devaluation have on a country's 'Terms of Trade' (ToT)?
Terms of trade deteriorate (turn adverse), because exports become cheaper relative to imports, requiring a larger volume of exports to purchase the same volume of imports.
Tap to view answer
Which complementary fiscal and monetary policies must a government adopt for devaluation to succeed?
Tight monetary policy (raising interest rates) and fiscal discipline (cutting the budget deficit) to suppress the inflationary pressures triggered by devaluation.
Tap to view answer
What is 'Competitive Devaluation' or a 'Currency War' (Beggar-Thy-Neighbour Policy)?
When one country devalues its currency and its competing trading partners retaliate by deliberately devaluing their own currencies to protect their exports.
Tap to view answer
What devastating impact did competitive devaluation have on global trade during the Great Depression of the 1930s?
Global trade collapsed completely, protectionism reached extreme levels, and all participating countries suffered severe economic losses.
Tap to view answer
What legal restriction is imposed on competitive devaluation under the Articles of the IMF?
Member countries are strictly prohibited from deliberately manipulating their currencies to gain unfair trade advantages or avoid BoP adjustment.

What major exchange rate allegation did the US and Western nations repeatedly level against China during the 2000s?
That China deliberately kept its currency, the 'Yuan' (Renminbi), undervalued to keep its massive manufacturing exports artificially cheap.

What positive impact does devaluation have on the Domestic Tourism industry?
Travelling to India becomes significantly cheaper and more attractive for foreign tourists, driving a surge in inbound tourism and forex earnings.

What impact does devaluation have on a country's Information Technology (IT) and Business Process Outsourcing (BPO) sector?
Highly beneficial; because their revenues are earned in dollars while most operating costs (engineers' salaries) are in rupees, boosting their operating profit margins.

What negative impact does devaluation have on the Aviation industry?
Airlines face acute distress because their largest expenses—Aviation Turbine Fuel (ATF) and aircraft lease rentals—are denominated in dollars, causing operating costs to spike suddenly.

What is the immediate psychological impact of devaluation on the domestic Stock Market?
Short-term panic and sell-offs are observed, as foreign investors fear an erosion in the dollar-denominated value of their portfolios.
Tap to view answer
What is the relationship between currency devaluation and 'Import Substitution'?
When foreign goods become excessively expensive, domestic manufacturers gain a strong economic incentive to produce indigenous substitutes for imported goods.

How can devaluation affect a country's Sovereign Credit Rating?
If devaluation stems from a deep structural crisis, international agencies like Moody's or S&P may downgrade the country's credit rating, making future borrowing costlier.

What is the Income Redistribution effect of currency devaluation across different sections of society?
Exporters, forex earners, and large industrialists benefit, whereas the fixed-salaried middle class and the poor suffer losses due to imported inflation.

Can merely devaluing a currency produce a permanent and sustainable boom in a country's exports?
No; devaluation provides only a temporary price advantage; long-term export success depends on global product quality, infrastructure efficiency, innovation, and logistics costs.

Which permanent structural reforms did India prioritize after 1991 instead of relying on devaluation?
Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes, Ease of Doing Business, modern ports and logistics (PM GatiShakti), and skill development.

What is 'Indirect Intervention' used by the RBI to curb excessive volatility in the forex market?
Hiking the policy repo rate, relaxing interest rate ceilings on non-resident deposits (FCNR) for banks, and raising foreign investment limits in government securities.

What does 'Forward Premium' indicate in the foreign exchange market?

What dual impact does a weakening rupee have on India's pharmaceutical sector?
Exports of generic drugs yield substantial dollar gains, while the import bill for raw materials (Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients — APIs) imported from China increases.

What was the historical concept of 'Elasticity Pessimism' in the context of exchange rates?
The scepticism among economists of the 1940s and 1950s that price elasticities of exports and imports in developing countries were too low for devaluation to ever fix a trade deficit.

Why is 'Elasticity Pessimism' considered flawed in modern economics?
Because in the long run, demand for both imports and exports has been found to be highly elastic, and volume adjustments inevitably occur over time.

Why has the Indian rupee depreciated against the US dollar over the long run despite periodic dollar sales by the RBI?
Due to the Structural Inflation Differential between India and the US; average inflation in India has historically been 3–4% higher than in the US.

According to Relative Purchasing Power Parity (Relative PPP), what is the inevitable exchange rate outcome of higher inflation in India?
A gradual nominal depreciation of the rupee against the dollar averaging 2% to 3% per year is economically natural and inevitable.

What would happen if the RBI attempted to keep the rupee artificially strong and prevented depreciation?
The rupee would experience Real Overvaluation, pricing Indian exports out of global markets, allowing cheap Chinese imports to destroy domestic industries, and exhausting forex reserves.

Why is maintaining a realistic, market-based level for the rupee a matter of survival for Indian manufacturing (Make in India)?
Because an overvalued currency makes foreign imports artificially cheap and destroys domestic manufacturing, whereas a competitive exchange rate provides a protective shield to domestic industry.

When Indian importers and exporters invoice and settle foreign trade directly in Indian rupees, they avoid the need to demand dollars or bear exchange rate fluctuation risks.

What is the final and enduring economic conclusion from the comprehensive analysis of currency devaluation, depreciation, and exchange rate management?
