Inflation Part 1

Definition of inflation, types based on rate and speed, Demand-Pull inflation, Cost-Push inflation, and macroeconomic impacts

🔍
Quick Reference
Key Facts & Contributions
Inflation Part 1
Type of InflationKey CausesEconomic Impact
Creeping InflationSlow and natural rise in general demand accompanying economic growth (typically below 3% per annum).Highly favourable and stimulating for the economy; provides profit incentives to producers and drives investment and employment.
Walking / Trotting InflationDemand growing slightly faster than supply as the economy expands (typically between 3% and 7% or 10% per annum).Acts as a warning signal; if timely monetary and fiscal measures are not taken, it can escalate into rapid inflation.
Running InflationAcute fiscal deficit, excessive money supply, and supply bottlenecks (typically double-digit rates of 10% to 20% per annum).Sharp erosion in the purchasing power of the middle class and salaried workers; discourages savings and increases the risk of capital flight.
Galloping & HyperinflationReckless printing of currency by the central bank to cover war, political collapse, or budget deficits (50%+ monthly or 1000%+ annually).Complete collapse of the national currency's value and public trust; breakdown of the medium of exchange, leading to barter and dollarization.
Demand-Pull InflationAggregate Demand exceeding Aggregate Supply ('Too much money chasing too few goods').Surge in output and profits in the short run, but once full capacity is reached, only prices rise; controllable via monetary tightening (repo rate hikes).
Cost-Push InflationSupply shocks caused by rising costs of raw materials (crude oil), wages, transport, indirect taxes, or natural disasters.Rising production costs push prices up while output and employment may fall; acts as the primary catalyst for 'Stagflation'.
Structural InflationInfrastructural deficiencies in storage, transport, cold chains, and marketing, along with institutional market failures in developing economies.Frequent supply bottlenecks in agriculture and essential commodities; cannot be solved by interest rate hikes alone—structural reforms are mandatory.
StagflationSimultaneous occurrence of high inflation, economic stagnation (recession), and high unemployment triggered by cost-push supply shocks (e.g., global oil crises).Failure of traditional Keynesian policies; curbing inflation worsens unemployment, while reducing unemployment fuels inflation.

One-Liner Questions

What is the standard and authoritative definition of 'Inflation' in macroeconomics?
A sustained and broad-based rise in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over time.

What is the direct effect of inflation on the 'Purchasing Power of Money'?
The purchasing power of money declines steadily; that is, a given unit of currency buys fewer goods and services than before.

Can a temporary price spike in one or two specific items (such as only tomatoes or onions) be termed inflation?
No; inflation is a sustained and economy-wide broad-based rise in prices, not a seasonal or transient price spike in a single commodity.

How did the renowned economist Crowther define inflation?
'Inflation is a state in which the value of money is falling, i.e., prices are rising.'

What was monetarist economist Milton Friedman's historic statement on inflation?
'Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon.'

What is 'Deflation' in an economy?
A persistent decline in the general price level (negative inflation rate < 0%), signalling a severe collapse in aggregate demand and economic recession.

What is the technical difference between 'Disinflation' and 'Deflation'?
Disinflation means a slowdown in the rate of inflation (e.g., falling from 8% to 4%—prices are still rising, but more slowly), whereas deflation means prices actually fall (the inflation rate turns negative).

What is the policy concept of 'Reflation' in an economy?
Deliberately using fiscal and monetary stimulus by the government and central bank to revive the price level and economic activity during a recession or deflation.
Tap to view answer
What is 'Headline Inflation'?
The broad inflation rate based on the total consumer or wholesale price index, which includes highly volatile items such as food and fuel.
Tap to view answer
What is the definition of 'Core Inflation'?
The underlying and stable rate of inflation obtained by stripping out highly volatile components—Food and Energy/Fuel—from Headline Inflation.
Tap to view answer
Why does the central bank (RBI) analyze Core Inflation alongside Headline Inflation when formulating long-term monetary policy?
Because Core Inflation reflects the true underlying demand pressures in the economy, free from transient noise caused by weather or global oil shocks.
Tap to view answer
What is 'Open Inflation'?
A situation where the government or central bank makes no administrative intervention to control the price level, and prices rise freely through market demand and supply.
Tap to view answer
What is meant by 'Suppressed / Repressed Inflation'?
When, despite intense demand pressures in the economy, the government artificially suppresses price rises through price controls, rationing, and administrative orders.
Tap to view answer
What is the negative consequence of maintaining Suppressed Inflation for a prolonged period?
It leads to severe market shortages, hoarding, long queues, and black marketing.
Tap to view answer
What is 'Sporadic / Sectional Inflation' in an economy?
When price increases are confined to a single specific sector or commodity group (such as only pulses or only petroleum) rather than spreading across the entire economy.
Tap to view answer
What is 'Comprehensive Inflation'?
When price increases spread simultaneously and broadly across all sectors and virtually all essential goods and services in the economy.
Tap to view answer
Based on speed, what is the threshold for 'Creeping Inflation'?
A very slow and gradual price rise, generally below 3% per annum.
Tap to view answer
Why is Creeping Inflation considered a 'tonic' or beneficial for any developing or developed economy?
Because it gives producers a modest profit margin, continuously incentivizing new investment, output expansion, and job creation.
Tap to view answer
What is the typical rate of 'Walking or Trotting Inflation'?
A moderate price rise ranging roughly between 3% and 7% or 10% per annum.
Tap to view answer
Why is Walking Inflation considered a 'Warning Signal' for policymakers?
Because if prompt monetary control is not exercised, it can rapidly escalate into uncontrolled double-digit Running Inflation.
Tap to view answer
What characterizes 'Running Inflation'?
When the rate of price increase reaches double digits, between 10% and 20% per annum.
Tap to view answer
How does Running Inflation affect the salaried middle class and the poor?
Their real income shrinks rapidly, savings begin to erode, and daily living becomes extremely arduous.
Tap to view answer
What is 'Galloping Inflation / Jumping Inflation'?
When inflation leaps from double digits into triple digits (20%, 50%, 100%, or even higher per annum).
Tap to view answer
What is the standard statistical definition of 'Hyperinflation' in economics?
According to economist Phillip Cagan, when the rate of inflation exceeds 50% per month (roughly 13,000% per annum).
Tap to view answer
What happens to a country's national currency during Hyperinflation?
The currency's value becomes practically zero (worthless paper); people refuse to accept the national currency, and the economy shifts to barter or foreign currencies (dollarization).

Which have been the most notorious global examples of Hyperinflation in history?
Weimar Germany after World War I (1923), Zimbabwe in 2008, and Venezuela in recent years.

What was the primary cause of Zimbabwe's 2008 hyperinflation crisis?
The collapse of agricultural output and reckless printing of billions and trillions of dollars by the central bank to finance the budget deficit.

What is the standard economic definition of 'Demand-Pull Inflation'?
When Aggregate Demand for goods and services in an economy grows faster than Aggregate Supply.
Tap to view answer
How is Demand-Pull Inflation described in a popular economic adage?
'Too much money chasing too few goods.'
Tap to view answer
On which famous economist's ideas is the primary theoretical analysis of Demand-Pull Inflation based?
On John Maynard Keynes's theory of the 'Inflationary Gap'.
Tap to view answer
According to Keynes, when does an 'Inflationary Gap' arise?
When aggregate planned expenditure (C + I + G + X) exceeds the total output achievable at the Full Employment level in the economy.
Tap to view answer
If demand rises further after reaching Full Employment, what happens to output and prices?
Physical output cannot increase further because all resources are already employed; hence, additional demand only pulls prices upward (pure Demand-Pull Inflation).
Tap to view answer
What are the major monetary factors that trigger Demand-Pull Inflation?
Rapid expansion of Money Supply (M3) by the central bank, excessive cuts in policy interest rates, and indiscriminate cheap credit disbursement by banks.
Tap to view answer
What are the key fiscal factors at the government policy level that fuel Demand-Pull Inflation?
A sharp rise in public expenditure, steep tax cuts (which boost disposable income), and a large fiscal deficit.
Tap to view answer
How does 'Monetization of Deficit' (printing currency) give rise to Demand-Pull Inflation?
To bridge the budget deficit, the government injects fresh purchasing power into the market without increasing real output, causing demand to outstrip supply.
Tap to view answer
How can heavy foreign capital inflows affect domestic Demand-Pull Inflation?
When the RBI buys dollars from the market during heavy FDI and FPI inflows and releases rupees, domestic money supply and liquidity expand.
Tap to view answer
How does a sharp 'Surge in Exports' induce Demand-Pull Inflation?
Selling domestic goods abroad reduces their availability in the domestic market, while rising incomes of exporters boost domestic demand.
Tap to view answer
What role do rapid population growth and urbanization play in Demand-Pull Inflation?
They continuously increase total consumption demand for foodgrains, housing, water, transport, and civic amenities.
Tap to view answer
What major measures does the central bank (RBI) adopt to control Demand-Pull Inflation?
Hiking the policy Repo Rate, raising the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR), and absorbing excess liquidity from the market by selling securities via Open Market Operations (OMO Sales).
Tap to view answer
What fiscal measures does the government take to curb Demand-Pull Inflation?
Cutting public expenditure, raising direct taxes to reduce people's consumption capacity, and narrowing the fiscal deficit.
Tap to view answer
What is the standard authoritative definition of 'Cost-Push Inflation'?
When rising costs of basic factors of production (raw materials, wages, energy, transport, taxes) shift the Aggregate Supply curve to the left, pushing prices upward.
Tap to view answer
By what other name is Cost-Push Inflation known?
'Supply-Shock Inflation'.
Tap to view answer
What is the most fundamental initial difference between Demand-Pull and Cost-Push Inflation?
Demand-Pull inflation originates from rising buyer demand, whereas Cost-Push inflation originates from rising production costs for producers or supply shortages.
Tap to view answer
What is considered the single most prominent and universal global cause of Cost-Push Inflation?
Sudden, sharp spikes in international prices of Crude Oil and natural gas.
Tap to view answer
What type of inflation did the historic 'OPEC Oil Crises' of 1973 and 1979 trigger globally?
Pure Cost-Push Inflation; quadrupling oil prices sent global manufacturing and transportation costs skyrocketing.
Tap to view answer
What is the 'Wage-Price Spiral' and how is it linked to Cost-Push Inflation?
When prices rise, workers demand higher wages -> higher wages raise production costs -> producers raise prices further -> this vicious cycle repeats continuously.
Tap to view answer
What is the concept of 'Imported Inflation' in an economy?
When imported raw materials (crude oil, edible oils, chemicals) become costlier internationally, or when depreciation of the domestic currency makes imports more expensive.

If the Indian Rupee depreciates from ₹80 to ₹85 against the US Dollar, which type of inflation will flare up in India?
Cost-Push Imported Inflation, because the rupee bill for imported crude oil and components will rise immediately.

How do natural disasters (severe droughts, floods, unseasonal rains) trigger Cost-Push Inflation?
Destruction of agricultural crops causes a sharp drop in the supply of foodgrains, vegetables, and pulses, causing food prices to spike suddenly.
Tap to view answer
What type of inflation is caused by a steep hike in indirect taxes (GST or excise duties)?
Cost-Push Inflation (Tax-Push Inflation), because higher taxes directly raise the final retail price of goods.
Tap to view answer
What is 'Profit-Push / Mark-up Inflation'?
When large corporate firms in monopolistic or oligopolistic markets abuse their market power to raise prices and expand their profit margins (mark-up) even without any rise in production costs.

Which Supply Chain Disruptions played a role in post-COVID-19 global inflation?
Port congestion, global container shortages, semiconductor chip scarcity, and a five-fold surge in ocean freight rates.

Why is monetary policy (raising interest rates) alone not fully effective in tackling Cost-Push Inflation?
Because raising interest rates dampens demand but cannot fix real supply shortages caused by oil crises, droughts, or wars; doing so increases the risk of recession.

What 'Supply-side Measures' does the government take to control Cost-Push Inflation?
Cutting excise duty on petrol and diesel, reducing customs duty on imports of essential commodities, releasing grain from buffer stocks into the open market, and cracking down on hoarding.

Which economists propounded the theory of 'Structural Inflation' in the context of developing countries?
Latin American economists Raul Prebisch and Celso Furtado.

According to Structural Inflation, what are the three main structural bottlenecks causing price rises in developing countries?
1. Agricultural bottleneck (lack of storage and irrigation), 2. Foreign exchange bottleneck (limited import capacity), and 3. Financial bottleneck (low tax revenue and poor infrastructure).

What is the definition of 'Stagflation' in economics?
An adverse economic condition in which high inflation, slow economic growth or stagnation, and high unemployment exist simultaneously.

Iain Macleod, in the British Parliament.

Why does Stagflation pose the toughest dilemma for economic policymakers?
Because increasing spending to fight recession and unemployment fuels inflation further, while raising interest rates to curb inflation deepens the recession.

When and why did Stagflation first emerge historically in the world?
In the 1970s in the US and Western nations following the OPEC oil shock, which shook the core assumptions of traditional Keynesian economics.

Which economist propounded the 'Phillips Curve' in 1958?
A. W. Phillips.

Between which two macroeconomic variables does the traditional Short-Run Phillips Curve show an inverse relationship?
Between the rate of inflation and the rate of unemployment; that is, reducing unemployment raises inflation, and reducing inflation raises unemployment.
Tap to view answer
How did the rise of Stagflation in the 1970s disprove the traditional Phillips Curve?
Because high inflation and high unemployment rose simultaneously during that period, causing the Short-Run Phillips Curve to shift rightward.

According to Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps, what is the shape of the 'Long-Run Phillips Curve'?
A completely vertical straight line, showing that there is no trade-off between inflation and unemployment in the long run.

What is the point at which the Long-Run Phillips Curve is vertical called in economics?

What is the definition of NAIRU (Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment)?
The lowest rate of unemployment at which the inflation rate remains stable in an economy without accelerating.

What is the concept of 'Inflation Tax'?
The erosion of the real purchasing power of cash held by the public due to inflation; it acts as an invisible tax that reduces the government's real liabilities and transfers wealth from the public to the government.

What is 'Seigniorage'?
The net financial profit earned by the government or central bank from the difference between the face value of currency and the actual cost of printing it.

During inflation, who gains and who loses between a 'Borrower (Debtor)' and a 'Lender (Creditor)'?
The borrower gains (as they repay the loan in cheaper money with eroded purchasing power), while the lender suffers a real loss.

How does inflation affect the 'Fixed-Income Group' (pensioners, salaried employees)?
They suffer the heaviest economic loss, as their nominal income remains fixed while the daily cost of living rises continuously.

How does inflation affect 'Businessmen, Industrialists, and Producers' (Producers / Equity Investors)?
They earn windfall gains in the short run, as prices of finished goods rise immediately while production costs (wages, rent) adjust more slowly.

What adverse impact does inflation have on a country's 'Imports and Exports' (Balance of Trade)?

What is the final and overarching macroeconomic takeaway from the entire study of Inflation Part 1?
Inflation is a double-edged sword: while controlled, predictable, creeping inflation (2-4%) incentivizes producers and serves as the lifeblood of economic growth, uncontrolled demand-pull or cost-push inflation destroys the purchasing power of the poor, domestic savings, and macroeconomic financial stability; hence, a precise balance of monetary discipline and efficient supply-side management is the true guarantee of sustainable economic development.
