Financial Market Part 1

Meaning of financial market, major segments, trading of assets, and an in-depth analysis of the money market (Call Money, T-Bills, CP, CD, Repo)

🔍
Quick Reference
Key Facts & Contributions
Financial Market Part 1
Financial Market ComponentKey FeatureEconomic Role
Money MarketView Answer Market for short-term financial assets (maturity up to 1 year); high liquidity, minimal default risk, and low transaction costs.View Answer Balancing short-term liquidity mismatches in the economy and serving as the primary channel for monetary policy transmission by the central bank (RBI).
Capital MarketView Answer Market for medium- and long-term financial instruments (over 1 year); trading of shares (equity) and debentures/bonds.View Answer Long-term fixed capital formation in the country, financing industrial projects, and converting household savings into productive investment.
Call/Notice Money MarketView Answer Inter-bank short-term borrowing market; collateral-free transactions for 1 day (Call Money) up to 14 days (Notice Money).View Answer Meeting daily CRR/SLR requirements by commercial banks and rapid inter-bank adjustment of short-term liquidity.
Treasury Bills Market (T-Bills)View Answer Zero-coupon short-term sovereign debt instruments issued by the RBI on behalf of the Government of India (91-day, 182-day, 364-day).View Answer Bridging the government's temporary cash mismatches without printing currency and serving as a risk-free short-term interest rate benchmark for the entire economy.
Commercial Paper (CP)View Answer Unsecured promissory notes issued by top credit-rated corporate companies (7 days to 1 year; minimum denomination of ₹5 lakh).View Answer Providing the corporate sector with cheaper, non-banking alternative financing for working capital compared to traditional bank loans.
Certificate of Deposit (CD)View Answer Negotiable instruments issued by scheduled commercial banks and AIFIs against term deposits (7 days to 1 year).View Answer Assisting banks in attracting wholesale deposits and overcoming liquidity shortages during periods of credit expansion.
Commercial Bills MarketView Answer Bills of exchange issued on the basis of actual credit purchase and sale of goods; a marketable instrument of trade credit.View Answer Providing immediate cash to suppliers through bill discounting and facilitating business working capital.
Repo & Tri-Party Repo (Repo & TREPS)View Answer Repurchase agreements backed by government securities as collateral; a secure short-term market centrally cleared by CCIL.View Answer Enabling secure short-term borrowing and investment for financial institutions and allowing the RBI to manage daily liquidity in the banking system (LAF).

One-Liner Questions

What is the standard authoritative definition of a 'Financial Market' in economics?
An institutional and technological platform where financial assets (shares, bonds, debentures, bills, currency, etc.) are created, bought, sold, and transferred.
Tap to view answer
What is the most fundamental economic function of a financial market?
'Financial Intermediation'; that is, mobilizing funds from surplus units (savers) toward deficit units (entrepreneurs/investors).
Tap to view answer
Who are referred to as 'Surplus Units' and 'Deficit Units' in a financial market?
Surplus units are households and institutions whose income exceeds their expenditure (savers), whereas deficit units are governments and firms that require additional capital for investment.
Tap to view answer
What is the significance of the 'Allocative Function' performed by financial markets?
It directs scarce capital resources into economic activities where the marginal productivity of capital and returns are highest.
Tap to view answer
How does a financial market facilitate the process of 'Price Discovery'?
By determining the true and fair market value of financial assets based on transparent market forces of demand and supply.
Tap to view answer
What is meant by providing 'Liquidity' to financial assets?
Offering investors the facility to convert their securities into cash immediately, in minimal time, and without any major loss of value whenever they wish.
Tap to view answer
How do financial markets reduce 'Transaction Costs' and information asymmetry?
By providing a common platform for buyers and sellers, which minimizes the time, effort, and cost of gathering information to find counterparties.
Tap to view answer
What are the four major pillars of a country's 'Financial System'?
1. Financial Markets, 2. Financial Intermediaries/Institutions, 3. Financial Assets/Instruments, and 4. Financial Services & Regulators.
Tap to view answer
What is the difference between 'Direct Finance' and 'Indirect Finance'?
In direct finance, borrowers raise funds directly from investors by selling shares/bonds in the financial market, whereas in indirect finance, intermediary institutions like banks collect funds from depositors and lend them to borrowers.
Tap to view answer
How does the development of financial markets accelerate a country's economic growth?
By mobilizing idle household savings and transforming them into large infrastructure, technological innovation, and productive industrial investment.
Tap to view answer
Based on maturity period, into which two main segments is the financial market divided?
1. Money Market (short-term, up to 1 year) and 2. Capital Market (medium- and long-term, more than 1 year).
Tap to view answer
Based on the issuance of securities, into which two parts is the financial market divided?
1. Primary Market (market for new issues) and 2. Secondary Market (market for previously issued securities).
Tap to view answer
What is the primary function of the 'Primary Market'?
Raising fresh capital directly for companies and the government by issuing new securities (IPO, FPO, Rights Issue).
Tap to view answer
What is the role of the 'Secondary Market' (Stock Exchange)?
Facilitating the buying and selling of already issued securities among existing investors and providing liquidity to them.
Tap to view answer
Based on the nature of claim, into which two forms is the financial market classified?
1. Debt Market (bonds, debentures, loans) and 2. Equity Market (shares, ownership stake).
Tap to view answer
What is the fundamental difference in an investor's rights between the Debt Market and the Equity Market?
In the debt market, the investor is a creditor with a legal right to fixed interest and principal repayment, whereas in equity, the investor is a proportionate owner of the company whose return depends on the company's profits.
Tap to view answer
What is the defining feature of an 'Organized Financial Market'?
A market that operates under formal legal rules, standardized procedures, and strict control of statutory regulators (such as RBI, SEBI).
Tap to view answer
What are the major components of the 'Unorganized Financial Market'?
Traditional moneylenders, mahajans, indigenous bankers, chit funds, and unregistered financial groups.
Tap to view answer
What is known as the 'Gilt-Edged Market'?
The market for Government Securities (G-Secs); they are called 'gilt-edged' (gold-edged/safest) securities because they carry zero risk of default.
Tap to view answer
What is the characteristic feature of the 'Derivatives Market'?
A financial market where financial contracts (Futures, Options, Swaps) are traded, whose value is derived from an underlying asset (shares, indices, currency, commodities).
Tap to view answer
What is the apex regulatory division of labor (Regulatory Architecture) for financial markets in India?
The RBI regulates the money market, government securities, and foreign exchange market; SEBI regulates the capital market, stock exchanges, commodities, and mutual funds.
Tap to view answer
Which institutions regulate the insurance market and the pension market in India, respectively?
The insurance market is regulated by IRDAI, and the pension market is regulated by PFRDA.
Tap to view answer
Which apex body establishes coordination among various financial regulators (RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA)?
The Financial Stability and Development Council (FSDC), chaired by the Union Finance Minister.
Tap to view answer
On the recommendation of which committee and in which year was the FSDC established?
On the recommendation of the Dr. Raghuram Rajan Committee (2008), in December 2010.
Tap to view answer
What is the definition of a 'Financial Asset' in economics?
An intangible, non-physical asset that represents future cash flows or economic value in the form of a contractual claim.
Tap to view answer
What is the difference between a financial asset and a physical asset?
A physical asset (such as land, buildings, machinery) is tangible and used directly in production, whereas a financial asset is merely a legal and financial claim.
Tap to view answer
What are the major examples of financial assets?
Bank deposits, shares, debentures, government bonds, treasury bills, commercial paper, and mutual fund units.
Tap to view answer
Under which Act was 'Dematerialization' (Demat) introduced in India in place of the 'Physical Form' of securities?
Under the Depositories Act, 1996.
Tap to view answer
What is a 'Demat Account'?
An electronic account in which an investor's securities (shares, bonds) are safely held in electronic/digital form instead of physical paper certificates.
Tap to view answer
Which are the two major national depositories operating in India?
1. NSDL (National Securities Depository Limited - 1996) and 2. CDSL (Central Depository Services Limited - 1999).
Tap to view answer
With the collaboration of which major institutions was India's first depository, NSDL, established?
With the joint collaboration of the National Stock Exchange (NSE), IDBI, and UTI (August 1996).
Tap to view answer
By which institution was CDSL primarily promoted?
By the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE) and major commercial banks (February 1999).
Tap to view answer
What is the role of a 'Depository Participant' (DP)?
An intermediary agent (such as a bank or brokerage firm) between the investor and the depository (NSDL/CDSL), through which an ordinary citizen opens a Demat account.
Tap to view answer
What is the difference between a 'Trading Account' and a 'Demat Account' in buying and selling financial securities?
A trading account is used to place orders to buy and sell shares in the stock market, whereas a Demat account is used to store purchased shares in electronic form.
Tap to view answer
What is the process of 'Clearing and Settlement' in the stock market?
Clearing calculates the obligations (net liability of funds and securities) of both parties after a trade, and settlement completes the actual payment of funds and actual transfer of shares.
Tap to view answer
Which settlement cycle is currently implemented as the standard in the Indian stock market?
The T+1 settlement cycle (full settlement on the next working day after the trade); additionally, T+0 (same-day settlement) has been launched on a pilot basis for select shares.
Tap to view answer
Which country became the second major country in the world to fully implement the T+1 settlement system?
India (the first major market after China to fully adopt T+1 in January 2023).
Tap to view answer
What is 'Counterparty Risk' in financial asset transactions?
The risk that the other party to a transaction (buyer or seller) may fail to fulfill its obligation (delivering funds or delivering shares) on time.
Tap to view answer
Who is responsible for eliminating counterparty risk in the stock market?
The 'Clearing Corporation' (such as NSCCL, ICCL), which acts as the Central Counterparty (CCP) and provides a 100% legal guarantee of settlement for every trade.
Tap to view answer
What does 'Rolling Settlement' mean in financial markets?
The sequential settlement of trades executed on each trading day separately, based on a fixed cycle of days (T+1).
Tap to view answer
What is the standard economic definition of the 'Money Market'?
A market for borrowing, investing, and trading short-term financial assets (whose original maturity period ranges from 1 day up to a maximum of 1 year).
Tap to view answer
What are the three key inherent characteristics of money market instruments?
1. Short-term maturity (up to 1 year), 2. High liquidity (immediate conversion to cash), and 3. Minimal default risk (Near-Money Assets).
Tap to view answer
Why are money market instruments called 'Near Money' in economics?
Because they are not legal tender themselves, yet they can be converted into cash immediately at very low cost and without any significant depreciation in value.
Tap to view answer
Which historic committee gave its recommendations in 1987 for the development and reform of the Indian money market?
The N. Vaghul Committee on Money Market.
Tap to view answer
Which new money market instruments and institutions were established based on the main recommendations of the Vaghul Committee (1987)?
Commercial Paper (CP), Certificate of Deposit (CD), Inter-Bank Participation Certificate (IBPC), and the establishment of DFHI.
Tap to view answer
What was the main objective of the 'Discount and Finance House of India' (DFHI), established by the RBI in 1988?
To provide continuous liquidity and discounting facilities in the secondary market for short-term money market instruments (T-Bills, commercial bills, etc.).
Tap to view answer
What is the biggest regulatory difference between the money market and the capital market?
The primary regulator of the money market is the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), whereas the primary regulator of the capital market is SEBI.
Tap to view answer
What is the nature of a 'Wholesale Market' in the money market?
Transactions occur in huge financial sizes (crores of rupees), and primarily banks, financial institutions, primary dealers, and large corporates participate in it.
Tap to view answer
Which money market interest rate indicator is the official 'Operating Target' of the RBI's monetary policy?
The Weighted Average Call Rate (WACR).
Tap to view answer
Why is the money market considered the first stage in Monetary Policy Transmission?
Because when the RBI changes the repo rate, its earliest and quickest impact falls on inter-bank call money rates and T-Bill yields in the money market.
Tap to view answer
Into which three segments is the inter-bank money market divided based on loan tenure?
1. Call Money (1 day / overnight), 2. Notice Money (2 to 14 days), and 3. Term Money (15 days to 1 year).
Tap to view answer
What is meant by 'Call Money' (Overnight Money)?
An inter-bank loan taken or given for a period of one business day (overnight) without any collateral (uncollateralized).
Tap to view answer
What is the tenure of 'Notice Money'?
A short-term loan given for a period ranging from 2 days to 14 days.
Tap to view answer
What is the time limit for 'Term Money'?
Inter-bank borrowing for a period exceeding 15 days and up to a maximum of 1 year.
Tap to view answer
To meet which statutory requirement do commercial banks primarily borrow from the call and notice money market?
To cover temporary shortfalls in the mandatory daily Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) and Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) requirements.
Tap to view answer
Which institutions are eligible to borrow and lend in the call money market?
Only scheduled commercial banks (including Regional Rural Banks), cooperative banks, and Primary Dealers (PDs).
Tap to view answer
Can Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs), mutual funds, and insurance companies borrow directly in the call money market?
No; to maintain the purity of the inter-bank market, the RBI has excluded non-bank entities from borrowing in the call money market (they can participate in Repo/TREPS).
Tap to view answer
How is the interest rate (Call Rate) determined in the call money market?
Entirely through competition between daily demand and supply of banks in the market (a highly volatile rate).
Tap to view answer
When there is a severe shortage of cash in the banking system, what is the impact on the call money rate?
The call money rate rises sharply above the policy repo rate and sometimes even touches the MSF rate.
Tap to view answer
Who sets the maximum borrowing limit (Prudential Limit) for a bank in the call money market?
The Reserve Bank of India (RBI), which is capped at a fixed percentage of the bank's paid-up capital and reserves.
Tap to view answer
What is the standard definition of 'Treasury Bills' (T-Bills)?
Short-term sovereign debt instruments issued by the Reserve Bank of India on behalf of the Government of India.
Tap to view answer
Can state governments issue Treasury Bills?
No; Treasury Bills are issued only by the Central Government (Government of India); state governments use Ways and Means Advances (WMA) and State Development Loans (SDLs).
Tap to view answer
Which three maturity tenures of T-Bills are currently issued regularly by the Government of India?
1. 91-day T-Bills, 2. 182-day T-Bills, and 3. 364-day T-Bills.
Tap to view answer
In what form is interest paid on T-Bills?
They are 'Zero-Coupon Securities'; no regular interest is paid on them, rather they are issued at a discount and redeemed at face value upon maturity.
Tap to view answer
If a 91-day Treasury Bill with a face value of ₹100 is issued at ₹98.20 and ₹100 is returned at maturity, what is the investor's return?
₹1.80 (the difference between the face value and the issue price is the investor's interest/yield).
Tap to view answer
What is the Minimum Investment Amount fixed for Treasury Bills?
A minimum of ₹25,000 and in multiples of ₹25,000 thereafter.
Tap to view answer
By whom and on which platform is the auction of T-Bills conducted?
By the RBI on its electronic core banking platform 'E-Kuber'.
Tap to view answer
What is the difference between 'Competitive Bidding' and 'Non-Competitive Bidding' in T-Bills auctions?
In competitive bidding, large institutional investors quote prices/yields, whereas in non-competitive bidding, retail investors and state governments receive T-Bills directly at the weighted average rate discovered in the auction.
Tap to view answer
Which portal has the RBI launched to enable retail investors to invest directly in T-Bills and government securities?
RBI Retail Direct Scheme (November 2021).
Tap to view answer
What are 'Cash Management Bills' (CMBs)?
Short-term T-Bills with a maturity period of less than 91 days, issued to meet immediate and unforeseen cash shortfalls of the Central Government.
Tap to view answer
In which year were Cash Management Bills (CMBs) first issued in India?
In the year 2010 (May 2010).
Tap to view answer
What were 'Ad-hoc Treasury Bills' discontinued in 1997, and why were they stopped?
Bills automatically purchased by the RBI by directly printing notes to cover budget deficits, which fueled uncontrolled inflation; they were discontinued under a 1997 agreement and replaced by WMA.
Tap to view answer
Are Treasury Bills recognized by banks for fulfilling their Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) mandate?
Yes; T-Bills are high-quality liquid government securities that are 100% eligible as SLR investments.
Tap to view answer
Why are T-Bills considered a 'Risk-free Asset' in the financial system?
Because they are backed by the sovereign guarantee of the Government of India, resulting in zero risk of losing principal or returns.
Tap to view answer
What is the standard definition of 'Commercial Paper' (CP)?
An unsecured promissory note issued by leading corporate companies with top credit ratings, primary dealers, and All India Financial Institutions to raise short-term working capital.
Tap to view answer
In which year and on whose recommendation was Commercial Paper (CP) formally introduced in India?
In the year 1990, on the recommendation of the Vaghul Committee.
Tap to view answer
What are the minimum and maximum maturity periods of Commercial Paper (CP)?
Minimum 7 days and maximum 1 year (from the date of issue).
Tap to view answer
What is the mandatory Minimum Denomination of Commercial Paper (CP)?
Minimum ₹5 lakh and in multiples of ₹5 lakh thereafter.
Tap to view answer
What financial eligibility conditions must a corporate company fulfill to issue CP?
The company must have a minimum tangible net worth of ₹4 crore, a sanctioned working capital limit from a bank, and its loan account must be classified as standard.
Tap to view answer
What is the minimum credit rating required from a SEBI-registered credit rating agency to issue CP?
A minimum short-term credit rating of 'A-2' or its equivalent.
Tap to view answer
In what format are Commercial Papers (CPs) issued and held?
In dematerialized (Demat) form through NSDL or CDSL depositories (as promissory notes).
Tap to view answer
Why is issuing CP more attractive for corporate companies than taking a working capital loan from a bank?
Because companies with high creditworthiness can raise funds from the market via CP at lower costs/cheaper interest rates compared to bank lending rates.
Tap to view answer
What is the role of an 'Issuing and Paying Agent' (IPA) in Commercial Paper?
A scheduled commercial bank that verifies all documentation for the CP issuance and acts as the payment agent.
Tap to view answer
What is a 'Certificate of Deposit' (CD)?
A legal and negotiable financial instrument issued by scheduled commercial banks and All India Financial Institutions (AIFIs) against a lump-sum amount deposited for a fixed period.
Tap to view answer
In which year was the Certificate of Deposit (CD) introduced in India?
In the year 1989 (on the recommendation of the Vaghul Committee).
Tap to view answer
Which institutions are eligible to issue Certificates of Deposit (CDs)?
All scheduled commercial banks (including Small Finance Banks, excluding RRBs and Payments Banks) and RBI-authorized All India Financial Institutions (NABARD, EXIM Bank, SIDBI, NHB).
Tap to view answer
What are the minimum and maximum maturity periods of a CD issued by commercial banks?
Minimum 7 days and maximum 1 year.
Tap to view answer
What can be the maturity period of a CD issued by All India Financial Institutions (AIFIs)?
Minimum 1 year and maximum 3 years.
Tap to view answer
What is the Minimum Denomination fixed for a Certificate of Deposit (CD)?
Minimum ₹1 lakh and in multiples of ₹1 lakh thereafter (as per revised RBI guidelines).
Tap to view answer
What is the biggest difference between a regular Bank Fixed Deposit (FD) and a Certificate of Deposit (CD)?
An FD is non-transferable, whereas a CD is a negotiable instrument that can be sold or transferred to another party in the secondary market before maturity.
Tap to view answer
Can banks grant loans against Certificates of Deposit (CDs) issued by themselves?
No; under RBI rules, banks cannot grant loans against their own CDs nor can they buy them back before maturity.
Tap to view answer
When do banks primarily resort to issuing Certificates of Deposit (CDs)?
When credit demand in the economy is very brisk while regular retail bank deposit growth is sluggish, banks issue CDs at higher rates to raise wholesale liquidity.
Tap to view answer
What are 'Commercial Bills' or 'Trade Bills'?
A formal Bill of Exchange drawn by a seller on a buyer based on the credit sale of goods and services in business transactions.
Tap to view answer
When does a Trade Bill convert into a 'Commercial Bill'?
When that trade bill is formally accepted or discounted by a commercial bank.
Tap to view answer
Which three major parties hold a legal role in a Bill of Exchange?
1. Drawer (the seller), 2. Drawee (the buyer/debtor), and 3. Payee (the recipient of payment).
Tap to view answer
What is the process of 'Bill Discounting'?
Without waiting for the maturity date, the seller hands the bill to a bank, and the bank deducts a nominal commission (discount) to make immediate cash payment; on maturity, the bank recovers the full amount from the buyer.
Tap to view answer
Which special platform was launched by the RBI for online discounting of trade bills of Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs)?
TReDS (Trade Receivables Discounting System).
Tap to view answer
What is the biggest benefit of the TReDS platform for the MSME sector?
Relief from delayed payments by corporate buyers, and access to immediate cash at the lowest interest rate through online bids from multiple competing financial institutions.

What is the authoritative definition of 'Repo' (Repurchase Agreement)?
A formal agreement under which one party sells approved securities (G-Secs) to another party for cash and simultaneously promises to buy them back on a specified future date at a pre-determined higher price.
Tap to view answer
What is 'Reverse Repo'?
The exact opposite perspective of Repo; under which one party lends cash to buy securities and enters into an agreement to sell them back in the future at a pre-determined price (safe investment of surplus cash).
Tap to view answer
What does the 'Repo Rate' signify in a repo agreement?
The difference between the sale price and the repurchase price of the securities, which effectively represents the interest rate paid by the borrower.
Tap to view answer
What is the use of Repo under the 'Liquidity Adjustment Facility' (LAF) in India?
The RBI provides short-term loans (Repo) to commercial banks against government securities as collateral to meet their daily liquidity shortfalls.
Tap to view answer
What is the difference between 'Market Repo' and 'RBI LAF Repo'?
LAF Repo is a policy transaction solely between the RBI and banks, whereas Market Repo is an open market for mutual collateralized borrowing among various financial institutions, banks, mutual funds, and primary dealers.
Tap to view answer
What is the role of a 'Haircut' (Margin) in a repo agreement?
The safety margin between the actual market value of the securities and the actual loan amount granted against them (e.g., lending ₹95 against a ₹100 bond = 5% haircut).
Tap to view answer
What is 'Tri-Party Repo' (TREPS), launched in 2018?
A collateralized repo market where a neutral third party (Clearing Corporation of India Limited - CCIL) acts as an intermediary to manage collateral selection, valuation, margin, and settlement.
Tap to view answer
Which old popular instrument in the Indian money market did Tri-Party Repo (TREPS) replace?
It replaced CBLO (Collateralized Borrowing and Lending Obligation) from November 2018.
Tap to view answer
What is the biggest advantage of the TREPS market for non-banking entities (such as mutual funds)?
They can lend their large surplus cash overnight at interest against the completely safe collateral of government securities without bank intermediation.
Tap to view answer
In which year was the Clearing Corporation of India Limited (CCIL) established?
In April 2001 (promoted by major banks and financial institutions with RBI support).
Tap to view answer
What backbone role does CCIL play in the money market and government securities market?
It acts as the 'Central Counterparty' (CCP) for all Repo, TREPS, foreign exchange, and government securities transactions, guaranteeing zero settlement risk.
Tap to view answer
What are 'Money Market Mutual Funds' (MMMFs / Liquid Funds)?
Mutual fund schemes that invest the money of retail and institutional investors exclusively in ultra-safe money market instruments (T-Bills, CP, CD, TREPS) with a maturity of up to 91 days.
Tap to view answer
Why are Liquid Funds so popular among retail and corporate investors?
Because they offer higher returns than a savings bank account, carry negligible risk of capital loss, and provide instant redemption within T+1 day.
Tap to view answer
Under whose regulatory supervision do Liquid Mutual Funds operate?
Under the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) (in coordination with RBI's money market guidelines).
Tap to view answer
What have been the historical structural weaknesses of the Indian money market?
Excessive bank-centricity of the call money market, an underdeveloped secondary market for commercial bills, and concentration of liquidity with a few large public sector banks.
Tap to view answer
What is the 'Standing Deposit Facility' (SDF) launched by the RBI?
A collateral-free facility launched in April 2022 under which banks can deposit their excess cash directly with the RBI without any government securities in exchange and earn interest.
Tap to view answer
What is the significance of the Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) in the money market?
In emergency situations when inter-bank liquidity dries up, banks can pledge their SLR securities to borrow overnight funds from the RBI at a penal interest rate higher than the repo rate.
Tap to view answer
What impact do money market interest rates have on the cost of credit in the country's entire economy?
Money market rates form the basis for banks' Marginal Cost of Funds-based Lending Rate (MCLR) and External Benchmark-based Lending Rates (EBLR), which determine interest rates on home loans, auto loans, and business loans for the general public.
Tap to view answer
What is the final macroeconomic conclusion from the comprehensive study of financial markets and the money market?
Financial markets are the lifeline of a nation, where the money market provides short-term financial stability and daily liquidity balance, while the capital market lays the foundation for long-term industrial growth; an efficient, transparent, and dynamic money market is the most effective medium for delivering the central bank's monetary policy to the last citizen.
