Fiscal Policy Part 1

In-depth macroeconomic analysis of the meaning and instruments of fiscal policy, government receipts (revenue and capital), primary deficit, and the interrelationships of fiscal deficit

🔍
Quick Reference
Important Facts & Contributions
Fiscal Policy Part 1
Fiscal ConceptKey FeatureFiscal Impact
Fiscal PolicyView Answer The government's overarching macroeconomic policy concerning taxation, public expenditure, and public debt.View Answer Regulating aggregate demand in the economy, cyclically stabilizing inflation and recession, and steering inclusive economic growth.
Revenue ReceiptsView Answer Government receipts that neither create any liability for the government nor cause any reduction in financial or physical assets.View Answer Primary basis for non-debt financing of the government's regular, day-to-day administrative operations, salaries, pensions, and public welfare schemes.
Capital ReceiptsView Answer Government receipts that either increase the government's liabilities/debt (such as market borrowings) or reduce its assets (such as disinvestment).View Answer Financing long-term capital formation (roads, railways, ports); excessive debt-creating capital receipts increase the future interest and debt burden.
Tax RevenueView Answer Compulsory statutory contribution paid by citizens and corporates to the government (direct taxes such as income tax/corporate tax, and indirect taxes such as GST).View Answer The government's most sustainable and non-debt resource; the tax-to-GDP ratio is a key barometer of a country's fiscal capacity and resource-mobilization strength.
Non-Tax RevenueView Answer Fees for public services, fines, dividends from the RBI and Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs), and interest received from states and foreign governments.View Answer Expanding budgetary resources without placing an additional burden on the tax system and ensuring fair financial returns on public investments.
Fiscal DeficitView Answer The difference between total government expenditure and total non-debt receipts; the government's total net borrowing requirement for a financial year.View Answer Key indicator of the overall economy's sovereign creditworthiness, interest rate levels, crowding-out of private investment, and future debt sustainability.
Primary DeficitView Answer The net deficit obtained by subtracting interest payments on past accumulated debt from the fiscal deficit (Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit - Interest Payments).View Answer Accurate measure of the actual budgetary imbalance arising from the government's policies in the current financial year; current fiscal stance free from past debt burden.
Zero Primary DeficitView Answer A fiscal situation where the primary deficit is zero, meaning the fiscal deficit is exactly equal to annual interest payments.View Answer It indicates that the government is meeting all primary administrative and developmental expenditures of the current financial year from its regular receipts, and borrowing is undertaken solely to pay interest.

One-Liner Questions

What is the standard authoritative definition of 'Fiscal Policy' in macroeconomics?
The policy of the government to achieve broader economic objectives through the management of its revenue (taxation), public expenditure, and public debt.

Who is the primary operator and policymaker of fiscal policy in India?
The Ministry of Finance, Government of India (Central Executive).

What is the most fundamental institutional difference between fiscal policy and monetary policy?
Fiscal policy is operated by the government through the budget, taxes, and expenditure, whereas monetary policy is operated by the central bank (RBI) through interest rates, money supply, and liquidity.
Tap to view answer
Which economist is regarded as the foremost pioneer of active fiscal policy in modern economics?
John Maynard Keynes, who established the necessity of government spending and fiscal intervention after the Great Depression of the 1930s.
Tap to view answer
What are the three main traditional instruments of fiscal policy?
1. Taxation, 2. Public Expenditure, and 3. Public Borrowing & Deficit Financing.
Tap to view answer
With what is fiscal policy most primarily and directly connected in the economy?
With influencing the 'Aggregate Demand' of the economy and the level of national income.
Tap to view answer
When and how does the government resort to 'Expansionary Fiscal Policy'?
During an economic recession or slowdown, by increasing government expenditure and cutting taxes so that demand and employment in the economy can be boosted.
Tap to view answer
Under what circumstances is 'Contractionary Fiscal Policy' used?
During excessive inflation (overheating), by reducing government expenditure and raising taxes so that excess demand and liquidity can be curbed from the market.
Tap to view answer
What is the concept of 'Counter-Cyclical Fiscal Policy'?
Moving opposite to the business cycle—that is, reducing spending during booms/inflation and increasing government spending by widening the deficit during recessions.
Tap to view answer
Why is 'Pro-Cyclical Fiscal Policy' considered harmful in developing countries?
Because in it, the government increases spending indiscriminately during a boom and drastically cuts spending when revenues fall during a downturn, thereby deepening the recession.
Tap to view answer
What does the 'Allocation Function' of fiscal policy ensure?
Balanced distribution of resources between private and public goods, and investing capital in underserved sectors such as health, education, defence, and infrastructure.
Tap to view answer
Which constitutional objective is fulfilled by the 'Distribution Function' of fiscal policy?
Reducing income and wealth inequality through progressive taxation (higher taxes on the rich) and targeted welfare expenditure for the poor (subsidies, rations).
Tap to view answer
What does the 'Stabilization Function' of fiscal policy do?
Protects the economy from extreme shocks of high inflation and unemployment and maintains a sustainable growth rate alongside price stability.
Tap to view answer
What are called 'Automatic Stabilizers' in fiscal policy?
Progressive income tax and unemployment allowances/welfare schemes, which automatically boost demand by widening the deficit during recessions and curb demand by raising tax revenue during booms without any new government legislation.
Tap to view answer
What is 'Discretionary Fiscal Policy' in fiscal policy?
Deliberate imposition of new taxes or announcement of special stimulus packages by the government through Parliament to deal with an economic crisis.
Tap to view answer
When does the 'Crowding-Out Effect' arise in the economy?
When the government borrows heavily from the market to bridge its large fiscal deficit, driving up market interest rates and making credit expensive and scarce for private industrialists.
Tap to view answer
What is the 'Crowding-In Effect'?
When government capital expenditure on core infrastructure such as highways, ports, railways, and power increases profitability and incentivizes private investment.
Tap to view answer
What is 'Fiscal Drag' in economics?
When inflation pushes citizens' nominal incomes up, automatically moving them into higher tax slabs (bracket creep), which reduces their real disposable income and slows down aggregate demand.
Tap to view answer
What is the value of the 'Balanced Budget Multiplier'?
Equal to unity (1); meaning if the government imposes new taxes of a certain rupee amount and spends the exact same amount in new public expenditure, national income rises by an amount equal to the tax/expenditure.
Tap to view answer
Through which constitutional process is the entire framework of fiscal policy presented every year in India?
Through the 'Annual Financial Statement' (Union Budget) presented in Parliament under Article 112 of the Constitution.
Tap to view answer
In the financial structure of the government budget, into which two main parts are 'Total Government Receipts' divided?
1. Revenue Receipts and 2. Capital Receipts.
Tap to view answer
What is the precise legal and economic definition of 'Revenue Receipts'?
Those government receipts that neither create any future financial liability for the government nor cause any reduction in any asset of the government.
Tap to view answer
Why are revenue receipts called non-redeemable?
Because the government has no legal obligation to return this amount to anyone.
Tap to view answer
Into which two major categories are revenue receipts divided?
1. Tax Revenue and 2. Non-Tax Revenue.
Tap to view answer
What is the authoritative definition of a 'Tax'?
A compulsory legal contribution imposed on citizens and enterprises under the authority of a law of Parliament or a State Legislature, in return for which no direct personal benefit (quid pro quo) can be claimed from the government.
Tap to view answer
Which Article of the Constitution specifies that 'No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law'?
Article 265 of the Constitution.
Tap to view answer
On the basis of incidence of taxation, into which two main types are taxes classified?
1. Direct Taxes and 2. Indirect Taxes.
Tap to view answer
What is a 'Direct Tax'?
A tax whose impact and incidence fall on the same person or entity; that is, the person on whom the tax is legally imposed bears the actual payment and cannot shift the tax burden onto others.
Tap to view answer
Which are the major direct taxes of the Central Government?
Personal Income Tax and Corporation Tax (Corporate Tax).
Tap to view answer
Which apex statutory body functions in India for the administration and collection of direct taxes?
The Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT), which functions under the Department of Revenue.
Tap to view answer
On what is 'Corporate Tax' levied?
On the net profit earned by companies and corporations during a financial year.
Tap to view answer
What is the identifying feature of an 'Indirect Tax'?
A tax whose initial impact falls on the seller or manufacturer, who then shifts its incidence onto the final consumer through prices.
Tap to view answer
Which are the major indirect taxes collected by the Central Government?
Central Goods and Services Tax (CGST), Integrated GST (IGST), Customs Duty, and Central Excise Duty on non-GST items (petroleum, tobacco).
Tap to view answer
Which apex body functions for the administration of indirect taxes and customs?
The Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC).
Tap to view answer
What is the difference between a 'Progressive Tax' and a 'Regressive Tax'?
In a progressive tax, the tax rate increases as income rises (such as income tax—heavier burden on the rich), whereas in a regressive tax, a proportionately larger share of a poor person's income goes toward tax (such as most indirect taxes).
Tap to view answer
What is a 'Degressive Tax'?
A tax where the rate initially rises with increasing income, but becomes flat after a certain threshold.
Tap to view answer
What is the difference between an 'Ad Valorem Tax' and a 'Specific Tax'?
An ad valorem tax is levied as a fixed percentage of the monetary value of a good (such as 18% GST), whereas a specific tax is levied on the physical unit, weight, length, or volume of a good (such as ₹19.90 per litre of petrol).
Tap to view answer
What is the technical difference between 'Tax Buoyancy' and 'Tax Elasticity'?
Tax buoyancy measures the automatic cumulative change in tax collection with GDP growth without adjusting for changes in tax rates, whereas tax elasticity isolates and measures the net impact after removing policy changes in tax rates.
Tap to view answer
What does the 'Tax-to-GDP Ratio' indicate?
Total tax collection as a percentage of the country's Gross Domestic Product, showing what share of the nation's total economic activity the government is able to mobilize as tax.
Tap to view answer
Approximately what is India's combined (Centre + States) Tax-to-GDP ratio at present?
Approximately 17% to 18% (the Centre's Tax-to-GDP ratio is ~11.5% to 12%).
Tap to view answer
What relationship does the Laffer Curve demonstrate between tax rate and tax revenue?
An inverted U-shaped relationship, showing that beyond a certain threshold (optimal tax rate), raising tax rates too high causes total tax revenue to decline due to tax evasion and disincentives.
Tap to view answer
Which major government income sources are included under 'Non-Tax Revenue'?
1. Surplus/dividends from the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) and financial institutions, 2. Dividends and profits from Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs), 3. Interest received on loans given to states and others, and 4. Fees, licence charges, and fines for government services.
Tap to view answer
Which is generally the largest single and unpredictable source of non-tax revenue in the Union Budget?
The annual dividend or surplus transfer made by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) to the Central Government.
Tap to view answer
Which high-level committee was constituted in 2018-19 to review the rules for surplus capital transfer by the RBI to the government?
Dr. Bimal Jalan Committee (Economic Capital Framework Committee).
Tap to view answer
Within what percentage range of total assets did the Bimal Jalan Committee recommend maintaining the RBI's 'Contingency Risk Buffer' (CRB)?
Within the range of 5.5% to 6.5%.
Tap to view answer
What is the major non-tax revenue received by the Central Government from the communications and telecom sector?
Spectrum Usage Charges (SUC), licence fees from telecom companies, and proceeds from 5G spectrum auctions.
Tap to view answer
How does 'escheat' (unclaimed property) add to non-tax revenue?
When a person dies without leaving a will and without any legal heir, the legal ownership of their entire property automatically passes to the government.
Tap to view answer
What is the standard definition of 'Capital Receipts'?
Those government receipts that either create a financial liability (debt) for the government or reduce the government's assets.
Tap to view answer
Into which two main types are capital receipts classified?
1. Debt-Creating Capital Receipts and 2. Non-Debt Capital Receipts (NDCR).
Tap to view answer
What are the major components of 'Debt-Creating Capital Receipts'?
Market borrowings (dated government securities and T-Bills), borrowings from the National Small Savings Fund (NSSF), external foreign loans, and short-term borrowings.
Tap to view answer
Which are the two most prominent examples of 'Non-Debt Capital Receipts'?
1. Recovery of old loans given to State Governments and PSUs, and 2. Disinvestment proceeds from the sale of government shares in PSUs.
Tap to view answer
Why is 'Disinvestment' in the public sector a non-debt capital receipt?
Because selling shares does not add any new debt onto the government (non-debt), yet the government's financial asset (shareholding) in the public enterprise decreases.
Tap to view answer
What is the equation for the government's 'Total Income' or 'Total Receipts' in the budget?
Total Receipts = Revenue Receipts + Capital Receipts.
Tap to view answer
How are the government's 'Non-Debt Total Receipts' calculated?
Non-Debt Receipts = Total Revenue Receipts + Non-Debt Capital Receipts (Loan Recovery + Disinvestment).
Tap to view answer
What is the special significance of non-debt receipts in budgetary analysis?
They reflect the government's actual own resources that can be spent without taking on any new debt.
Tap to view answer
What is the standard authoritative definition of 'Primary Deficit' in macroeconomics?
The difference obtained by subtracting net interest payments made by the government on previously accumulated debt from the fiscal deficit of the current financial year.
Tap to view answer
What is the standard formula for finding the Primary Deficit?
Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit - Net Interest Payments.
Tap to view answer
What does the concept of primary deficit reveal in budget analysis?
It shows the net deficit arising from the government's current fiscal policies and decisions (current spending and current income) in the current financial year.
Tap to view answer
Why is there a statistical and policy need to subtract interest payments from the fiscal deficit?
Because interest payments are a liability on accumulated debt taken by past governments; they are not a direct result of the current year's budgetary efficiency or current policy failure.
Tap to view answer
What is the real economic meaning of 'Zero Primary Deficit'?
The fiscal deficit is completely equal to annual interest payments (Fiscal Deficit = Interest Payments); meaning the government does not have to borrow to run its regular current-year expenses, and all new borrowing is going solely toward paying interest on old loans.
Tap to view answer
If a country's primary deficit becomes negative, what is it called?
'Primary Surplus'; it means the government's current non-interest revenue exceeds its current non-interest expenditure, and the government is paying a part of its interest payments from its current revenue.
Tap to view answer
What negative signal does a high primary deficit give about the economy?
The government is currently spending far beyond its resources, which will accelerate the accumulation of public debt in the future.
Tap to view answer
Can the primary deficit ever exceed the fiscal deficit?
No; under normal circumstances, since interest payment is always a positive number, the primary deficit will always be less than the fiscal deficit.
Tap to view answer
If the Government of India's fiscal deficit in a financial year is ₹16,00,000 crore and interest payments are ₹10,50,000 crore, what will be the primary deficit?
₹5,50,000 crore (16,00,000 - 10,50,000 = ₹5,50,000 crore).
Tap to view answer
What is the significance of the primary deficit as a percentage of GDP?
It shows international credit rating agencies (Moody's, S&P) and the IMF whether a country's current fiscal stance is sustainable or heading toward a debt crisis.
Tap to view answer
What central role does the primary deficit play in assessing 'Debt Sustainability'?
To stabilize or reduce the public debt-to-GDP ratio, any country must convert its primary deficit into zero or a 'Primary Surplus' in the medium term.
Tap to view answer
How is the 'Domar Condition of Debt Sustainability' related to the primary deficit?
If the real growth rate of the economy exceeds the real interest rate (g > r), the government can maintain a modest primary deficit and still keep its debt-to-GDP ratio stable.
Tap to view answer
What will be the outcome if the real interest rate exceeds the economic growth rate (r > g) and the country continues to run a primary deficit?
Public debt will grow explosively in an uncontrolled manner, and the country will inevitably fall into sovereign debt default or a debt trap.
Tap to view answer
What is the standard definition of 'Fiscal Deficit' in the context of the Union Budget?
The difference between the government's total expenditure (revenue expenditure + capital expenditure) and total non-debt receipts (revenue receipts + non-debt capital receipts).
Tap to view answer
Of which financial requirement of the government is the fiscal deficit truly a complete indicator?
The government's Total Net Borrowing Requirement from all sources for that financial year.
Tap to view answer
What are the two main sources for financing the fiscal deficit?
1. Domestic market borrowings and small savings (Internal Debt & NSSF), and 2. External Borrowings.
Tap to view answer
What is the mathematical and structural bridge between fiscal deficit and primary deficit?
'Interest Payments'; Fiscal Deficit = Primary Deficit + Interest Payments.
Tap to view answer
What does the wide gap between the fiscal deficit and the primary deficit in India's Union Budget indicate?
It indicates that a very large share of India's annual fiscal deficit (often more than 60% to 70%) is consumed solely in paying heavy interest on past accumulated debt.
Tap to view answer
When a government falls into a 'Debt Trap', how do the primary deficit and fiscal deficit behave?
Even though the primary deficit is low or zero, past interest payments become so massive that the total fiscal deficit keeps rising continuously, and the government has to take new loans just to pay old interest.
Tap to view answer
Which is traditionally the largest single expenditure component in the Government of India's annual revenue expenditure?
'Interest Payments', which consume about 35% to 40% of total revenue receipts and around 20% to 25% of total budgetary expenditure.
Tap to view answer
What two direct policy measures does the government have to control the primary deficit?
1. Rationalizing non-interest current expenditure (revenue and capital), and 2. Increasing non-debt receipts (tax compliance, widening the tax base, and disinvestment).
Tap to view answer
Can the government suddenly reduce its 'Interest Payments' in the current year?
No; interest payment is a 'Committed Expenditure' (mandatory contractual obligation) that can neither be deferred nor arbitrarily reduced.
Tap to view answer
If the government reduces the primary deficit to zero (Primary Deficit = 0), what will happen to the fiscal deficit?
The fiscal deficit will not disappear; it will still persist and will be exactly equal to the total interest payments due in that year.
Tap to view answer
What must the government achieve on the primary account to completely eliminate the fiscal deficit (Fiscal Deficit = 0)?
The government must generate a 'Primary Surplus' exactly equal in size to the interest payments.
Tap to view answer
What was the original statutory target of the 'Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act' (FRBM Act, 2003)?
To limit the fiscal deficit to 3% of GDP, completely eliminate the revenue deficit, and make public debt sustainable.
Tap to view answer
Under whose chairmanship was the FRBM Review Committee constituted in 2016-17?
Under the chairmanship of N. K. Singh.
Tap to view answer
What did the N. K. Singh Committee recommend adopting as the primary operational target for measuring fiscal discipline?
Making the 'Debt-to-GDP Ratio' the primary anchor instead of relying solely on the annual deficit.
Tap to view answer
What target did the N. K. Singh Committee set for general government debt (combined Centre + States) by 2023?
60% of GDP (of which Central Government debt was recommended at 40% and combined State debt at 20%).
Tap to view answer
What specific numerical targets did the N. K. Singh Committee suggest for the Central Government's fiscal deficit and primary deficit?
Bringing the fiscal deficit down to 2.5% of GDP and limiting the primary deficit to 0.2% to 0.0% (zero) of GDP.

What is the 'Escape Clause' (Section 4(2)) in the FRBM Act?
A legal provision allowing the government to deviate by up to 0.5% from the annual fiscal deficit target during national security threats, war, severe natural calamities, national-level agricultural crises, or structural reforms.

During the COVID-19 pandemic (FY 2020-21), to what percentage of GDP did the Government of India's fiscal deficit surge?
To 9.2% of GDP, the highest level in the modern history of independent India.

In the Union Budget 2021-22, by which financial year did the Finance Minister announce the Fiscal Consolidation Path to bring the fiscal deficit below 4.5%?
Bringing the fiscal deficit below 4.5% of GDP by FY 2025-26.

What does 'Revenue Deficit' indicate in the economy?
The excess of the government's regular revenue expenditure over its regular revenue receipts (Revenue Expenditure > Revenue Receipts).
Tap to view answer
Why is the persistence of a revenue deficit considered even more concerning for the economy than a fiscal deficit?
Because it means the government is borrowing even to meet its daily consumption, salaries, and administrative expenses without creating any asset (akin to borrowing to buy household groceries).

From which year was the concept of 'Effective Revenue Deficit' (ERD) introduced in the Union Budget?
From the Union Budget 2011-12 (during the tenure of Dr. Pranab Mukherjee as Finance Minister).
Tap to view answer
How is the Effective Revenue Deficit (ERD) calculated?
By subtracting from the revenue deficit those grants given by the Centre to the States for the creation of capital assets (roads, hospitals, school buildings).
Tap to view answer
Why is high growth in 'Capital Expenditure' (Capex) in the budget considered economically healthy?
Because Capex has a very high multiplier effect (~2.5 to 3.0), which attracts private investment and creates long-term productive assets.

To what level has the Government of India's Capital Expenditure (Capex) outlay been raised in the Union Budgets of FY 2024-25 and 2025-26?
To a historic high of ₹11.11 lakh crore (approximately 3.4% of GDP).

What was 'Monetized Deficit' in the government's overall deficit?
That portion of the fiscal deficit to finance which new currency notes were printed directly by the Reserve Bank of India (discontinued in 1997).
Tap to view answer
Why has direct monetization of the deficit been prohibited in modern economies?
Because it leads to an uncontrolled surge in money supply (M3), causing runaway inflation and a sharp depreciation in the external value of the currency.

What is meant by 'Fiscal Consolidation'?
The adoption of well-planned policies by the government to gradually bring down public debt and the fiscal deficit to a sustainable and safe level.
Tap to view answer
What are the two balanced pathways to achieve fiscal consolidation?
1. Revenue-led consolidation (increasing revenue by widening the tax base), and 2. Expenditure-led consolidation (trimming unnecessary unproductive subsidies and expenses).

What is the 'Public Account of India' (Article 266(2)) in government accounts?
The account in which the government holds public money as a banker or trustee (such as Provident Fund - PF, small savings, judicial deposits); withdrawing from it does not require an Appropriation Act of Parliament.

For what purpose is the 'Contingency Fund of India' (Article 267) used?
To draw advances with the President's permission to meet unforeseen and emergency expenditures (such as natural disasters), with ex-post facto approval obtained from Parliament later.

What funds are deposited into the 'Consolidated Fund of India' (Article 266(1))?
All revenues received by the government, all loans raised by the government, and all money received from the recovery of loans.

Article 114(3) of the Constitution (money can be withdrawn only after the Appropriation Bill is passed).

What is the special feature of 'Expenditure Charged on the Consolidated Fund'?
It can be discussed in Parliament, but no vote can be taken on it in the Lok Sabha (such as the President's salary, salaries of Supreme Court judges, and interest on government debt).

What is 'Off-Budget Borrowing' in fiscal policy, which has been eliminated in recent years?
Borrowing by Public Sector Undertakings (such as FCI) from the NSSF or banks outside the government budget to fund government schemes, so that the fiscal deficit appears smaller in the budget.

By directly incorporating the food subsidy loans taken by FCI into the official expenditure and fiscal deficit of the Union Budget.
Tap to view answer
What is considered 'Quality of Fiscal Adjustment' in fiscal policy?
Reducing the deficit not by cutting capital expenditure, but by controlling unproductive revenue expenditure and increasing tax revenue.
Tap to view answer
Of which positive structural shift in the economy is a steady decline in the primary deficit proof?
That the government's current income is becoming self-sufficient for its current operations and the future debt burden is on a downward trajectory.

What is the final and comprehensive macroeconomic conclusion of studying fiscal policy, government revenue, primary deficit, and fiscal deficit?
Fiscal policy is the mirror of economic sovereignty; while tax and non-tax revenues determine the state's self-reliance, scientific management of the fiscal deficit and primary deficit ensures that present development does not burden future generations with unbearable interest and a debt crisis.
