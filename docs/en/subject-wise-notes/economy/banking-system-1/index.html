Banking System Part 1

Comprehensive analysis of the structure of the Indian banking system, the monetary and regulatory role of the RBI, commercial banks, and public sector banks

Key Facts & Contributions
Banking System Part 1
Banking ComponentPrimary RoleEconomic Significance
Reserve Bank of India (RBI)View Answer The country's central bank; conducts monetary policy, issues currency, manages foreign exchange reserves, and regulates and supervises the entire banking system.View Answer Maintaining price stability, supporting economic growth, ensuring public confidence in the financial system, and acting as the 'Lender of Last Resort'.
Scheduled Commercial Banks (SCBs)View Answer Banks included in the Second Schedule of the RBI Act, 1934; accept demand and time deposits from the public, disburse loans, and perform credit creation.View Answer The main axis of financial intermediation in the economy; channeling savings into productive capital formation and operating the country's payment and settlement system.
Public Sector Banks (PSBs)View Answer Banks in which the government holds 51% or more stake (currently 12 PSBs including SBI); social and commercial banking through an extensive branch network.View Answer Concessional credit to the priority sector (agriculture, MSMEs), financial inclusion (PMJDY), Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT), and expanding banking access to remote areas.
Private Sector BanksView Answer Banks operated by private shareholders; high technology, professional management, innovation, and customer-centric banking services.View Answer Promoting healthy competition in the banking sector, improving service quality, rapid spread of digital products, and strong growth in corporate and retail credit.
Foreign BanksView Answer Banks incorporated in other countries that operate in India through branches or Wholly Owned Subsidiaries (WOS).View Answer Trade finance, cross-border capital flows, corporate banking for multinational companies, and introduction of global best regulatory practices.
Regional Rural Banks (RRBs)View Answer Banks jointly owned by the Centre, State, and Sponsor Bank (50:15:35); specifically focused on rural and semi-urban areas.View Answer Empowering the rural economy by providing institutional credit and banking facilities to small and marginal farmers, agricultural laborers, and rural artisans.
Cooperative BanksView Answer Based on 'One Member, One Vote' and 'No Profit, No Loss' principles; urban (UCBs) and rural three-tier short-term and long-term structures.View Answer Democratic financial services at the local level, micro-credit delivery, and freeing weaker sections from exploitation by moneylenders.
Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC)View Answer Wholly owned subsidiary of the RBI; provides statutory insurance for depositors' funds in case a bank fails.View Answer Maintaining unwavering public trust in the banking system and preventing bank runs by providing protection up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank.

One-Liner Questions

What is the authentic legal definition of 'Banking' under Section 5(b) of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949?
Accepting, for the purpose of lending or investment, of deposits of money from the public, repayable on demand or otherwise, and withdrawable by cheque, draft, order, or otherwise.

In modern economics, what are the two mandatory preconditions for a financial institution to be considered a 'Bank'?

How do Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs) differ from commercial banks?

Into which two broad categories is the Indian banking structure primarily divided?
1. Scheduled Banks, and 2. Non-Scheduled Banks.
Tap to view answer
What is the statutory definition of a 'Scheduled Bank'?
A bank that is included in the 'Second Schedule' of the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934.
Tap to view answer
What two mandatory conditions must a bank fulfill to be included in the Second Schedule of the RBI Act, 1934?
1. Its paid-up capital and reserves must have an aggregate value of not less than ₹5 lakh (currently ₹500 crore), and 2. Its affairs must not be conducted in a manner detrimental to the interests of its depositors.
Tap to view answer
What special regulatory facilities do Scheduled Banks receive from the Reserve Bank of India?
The right to obtain financial refinance/loans from the RBI at Bank Rate and Repo Rate, and automatic membership of the Clearing House.
Tap to view answer
What are 'Non-Scheduled Banks'?
Banks not included in the Second Schedule of the RBI Act, 1934; they do not get refinance facilities from the RBI under normal circumstances.
Tap to view answer
Which two major Acts govern the regulatory and supervisory framework of the Indian banking system?
1. Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 (RBI Act, 1934), and 2. Banking Regulation Act, 1949 (BR Act, 1949).
Tap to view answer
Under what name was the Banking Regulation Act (BR Act), 1949 originally enacted?
The Banking Companies Act, 1949; it was amended in March 1966 and renamed the 'Banking Regulation Act, 1949'.
Tap to view answer
Under Section 22 of the BR Act, 1949, whose permission is mandatory for any bank to commence business in India?
Obtaining a banking license from the Reserve Bank of India (RBI).
Tap to view answer
Into which four categories are Scheduled Commercial Banks (SCBs) structurally classified?
1. Public Sector Banks, 2. Private Sector Banks, 3. Foreign Banks, and 4. Regional Rural Banks (RRBs).
Tap to view answer
What is the fundamental structural difference between Commercial Banks and Cooperative Banks?
Commercial banks operate as joint-stock companies for profit, whereas cooperative banks operate on the principles of mutual cooperation, 'One Member-One Vote', and service.
Tap to view answer
Who currently holds regulatory control over Primary (Urban) Cooperative Banks (UCBs) in India?
After the Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2020, their entire banking regulation and supervision falls directly under the RBI.
Tap to view answer
In how many tiers is the rural cooperative credit structure organized?
A three-tier structure for short-term credit: 1. State Cooperative Banks (apex), 2. District Central Cooperative Banks (middle), and 3. Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS - village level).
Tap to view answer
Are Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS) directly regulated by the Reserve Bank of India?
No; PACS do not fall directly under the Banking Regulation Act; they are administered by the respective State Cooperative Departments and refinanced by NABARD.
Tap to view answer
Under the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC) Act, 1961, how much insurance cover is currently provided per depositor?
Principal and interest protection up to a maximum of ₹5,00,000 (five lakh rupees) per depositor per bank (increased from ₹1 lakh to ₹5 lakh in February 2020).
Tap to view answer
Who pays the premium for DICGC insurance?
The concerned bank itself pays it (12 paise per ₹100 of deposits annually); no fee is charged to depositors.
Tap to view answer
According to the DICGC (Amendment) Act, 2021, within how many days must depositors receive their insured amount up to ₹5 lakh if a moratorium is imposed on a bank?
Within a maximum of 90 days.
Tap to view answer
Are deposits in Indian branches of foreign banks operating in India also insured by DICGC?
Yes, deposits of all commercial banks operating in India (public, private, foreign, RRBs) are insured by DICGC.
Tap to view answer
Which deposits are explicitly excluded from the purview of DICGC insurance?
Deposits of foreign governments, deposits of Central/State governments, inter-bank deposits, and deposits of State Land Development Banks.
Tap to view answer
How does 'Branch Density', which measures the penetration of the banking system in India, compare between independence/nationalization and the present?
In 1969, there was one bank branch per 64,000 people, which has now improved to one branch per 9,500 to 10,000 people.
Tap to view answer
What is meant by 'Financial Inclusion' in the banking system?
Ensuring universal access to formal financial services (savings, credit, insurance, pension) at an affordable cost for disadvantaged, poor, and low-income groups.
Tap to view answer
What is the modified modern form of the 'No-Frills Account' system launched for banking expansion in India?
Basic Savings Bank Deposit Account (BSBDA), which has no minimum balance requirement.
Tap to view answer
Which arrangement was introduced by the RBI in 2006 to deliver banking services in unbanked rural areas?
The Business Correspondent (BC / Bank Mitra) model.

Which Royal Commission recommended the establishment of a Central Bank in India?
Royal Commission on Indian Currency and Finance (Hilton Young Commission - 1926).

Apart from the Hilton Young Commission, which other committee emphasized the necessity of a central bank in India?
The Central Banking Enquiry Committee (1931).

On which historic date and under which Act was the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) established?
On April 1, 1935, under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934.

Where was the central office of the RBI initially established, and when was it permanently shifted to Mumbai?
It was initially established in Calcutta (Kolkata) and permanently shifted to Mumbai in 1937.
Tap to view answer
What was the ownership structure of the RBI at the time of its establishment?
It was entirely a Private Shareholders' Bank with an authorized capital of ₹5 crore.

Under which Act and when was the Reserve Bank of India nationalized?
On January 1, 1949, under the Reserve Bank (Transfer to Public Ownership) Act, 1948.

Who was the first Governor of the Reserve Bank of India?
Sir Osborne Smith (from 1935 to 1937).
Tap to view answer
Who was the first Governor of Indian origin of the Reserve Bank of India?
Sir C. D. Deshmukh (Chintaman Dwarkanath Deshmukh - from 1943 to 1949).

According to the Preamble of the RBI, what is its core mandate?
To regulate the issue of bank notes and keeping of reserves with a view to securing monetary stability in India, and to maintain price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth.
Tap to view answer
By whom are the general superintendence and direction of the affairs of the Reserve Bank of India exercised?
By the Central Board of Directors of the RBI, comprising official and non-official directors.
Tap to view answer
What is the maximum number of Deputy Governors permissible on the Central Board of Directors of the RBI?
A maximum of 4 Deputy Governors.
Tap to view answer
Under Section 22 of the RBI Act, 1934, who holds the exclusive statutory monopoly to issue currency notes in India?
Only the Reserve Bank of India (RBI).
Tap to view answer
By whom are ₹1 notes and coins of all denominations issued in India?
By the Government of India (Ministry of Finance); the ₹1 note bears the signature of the Finance Secretary, although their circulation and distribution are handled by the RBI.
Tap to view answer
Whose signature appears on all bank notes of ₹2 and above?
The Governor of the Reserve Bank of India.
Tap to view answer
Which Note Issue System does the RBI currently follow?
Minimum Reserve System (MRS), which was adopted in 1956.
Tap to view answer
Under the Minimum Reserve System (MRS), what minimum reserve assets must the RBI always maintain?
Total assets of ₹200 crore, of which at least ₹115 crore in Gold and ₹85 crore in Foreign Currency Assets are legally mandatory.
Tap to view answer
Which Note Issue System did the RBI follow prior to 1956?
Proportional Reserve System, under which at least 40% of total notes issued had to be backed by gold and foreign securities.
Tap to view answer
Why is the RBI called the 'Banker to the Government'?
Because the RBI manages the banking accounts of the Central and State governments, manages their public debt, and provides them Ways and Means Advances (WMA).
Tap to view answer
What are Ways and Means Advances (WMA)?
Short-term loans (for a maximum of 90 days) provided by the RBI to bridge temporary cash-flow mismatches between the government's receipts and payments.
Tap to view answer
Why is the RBI called the 'Banker's Bank'?
Because all scheduled banks hold their statutory cash reserves (CRR) with the RBI, inter-bank claims are settled through the RBI, and the RBI lends to them during crises.
Tap to view answer
What is the role of the RBI as the 'Lender of Last Resort' (LOLR)?
When a solvent bank fails to obtain financial liquidity from any other market source, the RBI provides emergency liquidity assistance to prevent financial collapse and panic.
Tap to view answer
In which year was the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) constituted under Section 45ZB of the RBI Act, 1934?
In 2016 (based on the recommendations of the Dr. Urjit Patel Committee).
Tap to view answer
What is the total membership and composition of the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)?
6 members in total: 3 members from the RBI (RBI Governor, Deputy Governor, and 1 officer) and 3 independent external members appointed by the Government of India.
Tap to view answer
Who chairs the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), and what is the provision in case of a tie?
The RBI Governor is the ex-officio Chairperson; in the event of a tie, the Governor holds a Casting Vote.
Tap to view answer
What is India's official inflation target under the Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT) framework?
4% Consumer Price Index (CPI)-based inflation, with a tolerance band of +/- 2% (2% to 6%).
Tap to view answer
For how long must inflation remain below 2% or above 6% before the RBI is required to submit a formal report of failure to the government?
For Three Consecutive Quarters.
Tap to view answer
What is the standard definition of the Repo Rate (Repurchase Option)?
The interest rate at which commercial banks borrow short-term liquidity from the RBI by pledging government securities as collateral.
Tap to view answer
What is the Reverse Repo Rate?
The interest rate at which the RBI absorbs short-term surplus liquidity from commercial banks against government securities.
Tap to view answer
In what capacity was the Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) introduced by the RBI in 2022?
As a collateral-free instrument to absorb excess liquidity from banks without pledging government securities, becoming the new floor of the Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF).
Tap to view answer
What is the Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)?
A penal overnight borrowing window under which scheduled banks can borrow from the RBI during an emergency cash crunch by dipping into and pledging securities from their SLR quota.
Tap to view answer
What is the difference between the Bank Rate and the Repo Rate?
The Repo Rate is a collateral-based rate for short-term borrowing, whereas the Bank Rate is a collateral-free/penal rate for long-term lending (currently aligned with the MSF rate).
Tap to view answer
What are Open Market Operations (OMOs)?
Outright purchase or sale of Government Securities (G-Secs) by the RBI in the open market to regulate the money supply in the economy.
Tap to view answer
What is the impact on market liquidity when the RBI sells government securities in the open market?
Liquidity is absorbed from the market (liquidity contracts), reducing money supply and curbing inflation.
Tap to view answer
What is the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)?
A fixed percentage of its total Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) that every commercial bank must mandatorily hold as cash with the RBI.
Tap to view answer
Does the RBI pay any interest to banks on the cash held as CRR?
No; no interest is paid by the RBI on CRR balances.
Tap to view answer
What is the definition of the Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)?
A fixed percentage of its total NDTL that every bank must mandatorily maintain with itself in liquid form—such as gold, cash, or approved government securities (G-Secs).
Tap to view answer
What important supervisory power does the RBI hold under Section 35 of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949?
The power to conduct surprise or periodic physical inspections of the branches, books of accounts, and financial transactions of any commercial bank.
Tap to view answer
What stringent power does the RBI possess against bank management under Section 36AA of the BR Act, 1949?
The power to remove the Chairman, Director, CEO, or any other officer of a bank in the public interest or in the interest of the bank.
Tap to view answer
In which year was the RBI's 'Board for Financial Supervision' (BFS) established?
In 1994 (on the recommendation of the Narasimham Committee-I) for integrated supervision of commercial banks, financial institutions, and NBFCs.
Tap to view answer
Who is the custodian of the country's foreign exchange reserves under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999?
The Reserve Bank of India (RBI).
Tap to view answer
What is the primary economic function of Commercial Banks?
Mobilizing idle deposits from savers and channeling them as loans to productive investors and businesses (financial intermediation).
Tap to view answer
Which are the two main types of deposits accepted by commercial banks?
1. Demand Deposits (Current Account and Savings Account), and 2. Time/Term Deposits (Fixed Deposit and Recurring Deposit).
Tap to view answer
What is the fundamental difference between a Current Account and a Savings Account?
A Current Account is meant for businesses, earns no interest, and has no withdrawal limits, whereas a Savings Account is meant for personal savings and earns interest.
Tap to view answer
What is the CASA Ratio (Current Account Savings Account Ratio)?
The percentage of Current Account and Savings Account deposits in a bank's total deposits (CASA / Total Deposits × 100).
Tap to view answer
Why is a High CASA Ratio considered highly profitable for a bank?
Because zero interest is paid on current accounts and very low interest on savings accounts, significantly lowering the bank's overall Cost of Funds.
Tap to view answer
How do commercial banks perform 'Credit Creation' in an economy?
By keeping a fraction of primary deposits as cash reserves and repeatedly lending out the remainder as fresh loans to generate secondary/derivative deposits.
Tap to view answer
What is the theoretical formula for calculating the Money Multiplier (m)?
m = 1 / r (where r is the mandatory Legal Reserve Ratio / LRR).
Tap to view answer
If the Legal Reserve Ratio (LRR) is 10% and the initial primary deposit is ₹1,000 crore, how much total credit can the banking system create?
A total of ₹10,000 crore (1,000 × 1/0.10).
Tap to view answer
What constitute 'Assets' on a bank's balance sheet?
All loans and advances extended by the bank, investments in government securities, cash balances, and physical buildings/equipment—from which the bank earns income.
Tap to view answer
What constitute 'Liabilities' on a bank's balance sheet?
All customer deposits (Demand & Time Deposits), borrowings from other banks and the RBI, and share capital—which the bank must repay in the future.
Tap to view answer
How is Net Interest Income (NII) calculated?
NII = Total interest income earned by the bank on loans and advances - Total interest expense paid to depositors and on borrowings.
Tap to view answer
What does the Net Interest Margin (NIM) indicate?
The ratio of a bank's net interest income to its total interest-earning assets (NII / Total Earning Assets × 100).
Tap to view answer
What are the major sources of 'Non-Interest Income' (Fee-based Income) for commercial banks?
Commissions, brokerage, locker rent, foreign exchange conversion fees, loan processing fees, and fees from distributing mutual fund/insurance products.
Tap to view answer
What is meant by Capital to Risk-Weighted Assets Ratio (CRAR)?
The ratio of a bank's total capital (Tier-1 + Tier-2) to its Risk-Weighted Assets.
Tap to view answer
What is meant by Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA)?
The total value of a bank's various loans and assets measured by assigning different percentage risk weights based on their default risk (e.g., 0% risk weight on government securities, versus 100–125% on unsecured personal loans).
Tap to view answer
Under Basel-III norms, what is the minimum mandatory CRAR for Indian Scheduled Commercial Banks?
Minimum 9.0% (compared to the international Basel norm of 8.0%), and a total of 11.5% including the 2.5% Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB).
Tap to view answer
What does Tier-1 Capital (Core Capital) primarily include?
Paid-up equity share capital, statutory reserves, and retained earnings, which can absorb unexpected losses without halting the bank's operations.
Tap to view answer
Which components fall under Tier-2 Capital (Supplementary Capital)?
Revaluation reserves, general provisions, and long-term subordinated debt.
Tap to view answer
What is the objective of the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)?
To ensure that a bank holds sufficient High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) to survive an acute 30-day liquidity stress scenario.
Tap to view answer
Over what time horizon does the Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR) regulate structural liquidity risk?
Long-term liquidity risk over a horizon of at least 1 year.
Tap to view answer
What is the overall Priority Sector Lending (PSL) target for domestic scheduled commercial banks?
40% of Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC) or Credit Equivalent of Off-Balance Sheet Exposure, whichever is higher.
Tap to view answer
What is the mandatory sub-target for agriculture within the 40% PSL quota for domestic commercial banks?
18% of total ANBC (of which 10% is dedicated to small and marginal farmers).
Tap to view answer
What are the sub-targets fixed for Micro Enterprises and Weaker Sections under PSL?
7.5% for Micro Enterprises and 12% for Weaker Sections.
Tap to view answer
What punitive action does a commercial bank face if it fails to meet its mandatory 40% PSL target?
It must deposit the shortfall amount at low interest into the 'Rural Infrastructure Development Fund' (RIDF) managed by NABARD or into SIDBI/MUDRA funds.
Tap to view answer
What are Priority Sector Lending Certificates (PSLCs)?
A market-based tradable instrument allowing banks that exceed their PSL targets to sell their surplus achievement to banks falling short, without transferring the underlying loans.
Tap to view answer
What is the 'Letter of Credit' (LC) facility provided by commercial banks to customers?
A financial guarantee document in which the bank assures the seller that if the buyer fails to pay, the bank itself will make the payment.
Tap to view answer
What is the difference between a Bank Guarantee (BG) and a Letter of Credit (LC)?
In an LC, the bank is the primary payer, whereas in a BG, the bank pays only if the original customer defaults on the contract.
Tap to view answer
What are Public Sector Banks (PSBs)?
Commercial banks in which the Government of India or public institutions hold 51% or more equity shareholding.
Tap to view answer

What is the 'Overdraft' (OD) facility provided by commercial banks to customers?
A short-term credit facility (primarily on current accounts) allowing an account holder to withdraw funds beyond their actual available deposit balance up to a pre-approved limit.
Tap to view answer
What does the 'Credit-Deposit Ratio' (CD Ratio) indicate for commercial banks in India?
What percentage of the total deposits mobilized by a bank has been disbursed as loans.
Tap to view answer
What regulatory risk does a bank face if its CD ratio becomes excessively high (e.g., above 80–85%)?
Risk of a Liquidity Crunch, as credit growth outpacing deposit growth can lead to a cash shortage.
Tap to view answer
What is meant by the 'Chinese Wall' (Firewall) concept in banks' internal controls?

What does the 'Loan-to-Value Ratio' (LTV) determine in commercial banks?
The maximum percentage of loan a bank can sanction against the total market value of a pledged asset (e.g., 75–80% LTV on home loans).

Tap to view answer
What does the RBI expect from banks under 'Moral Suasion'?
