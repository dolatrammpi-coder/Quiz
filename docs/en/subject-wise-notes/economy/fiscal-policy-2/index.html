Fiscal Policy Part 2

Comprehensive analysis of the FRBM Act 2003, fiscal discipline, deficit financing mechanisms, the N. K. Singh Committee, and budgetary transparency

🔍
Quick Reference
Important Facts & Contributions
Fiscal Policy Part 2
Fiscal MechanismKey ProvisionImpact on Financial Discipline
FRBM Act, 2003 (Original Framework)View Answer Capping the fiscal deficit at 3% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and completely eliminating (0%) the revenue deficit by 31 March 2008 (later extended to 2009).View Answer Placed a statutory check on ad-hoc budgetary spending in India for the first time; mandated inter-generational equity and medium-term financial planning.
N. K. Singh Committee Review (2017)View Answer Bringing the debt-to-GDP ratio to 60% (Centre 40%, States 20%), reducing the fiscal deficit to 2.5%, and establishing a 'Fiscal Council'.View Answer Moved beyond narrow traditional deficit targets and made sovereign debt sustainability the primary anchor of India's fiscal policy.
Deficit Financing: Market BorrowingView Answer Raising funds from financial institutions, banks, and provident funds through open auctions of government bonds (dated securities and T-Bills).View Answer Controlled inflation by halting currency printing and deepened financial markets, though excessive borrowing risks crowding out the private sector.
End of Monetized Deficit (1997)View Answer Historic 1997 agreement between the RBI and the Central Government; complete ban on printing notes directly via Ad-hoc Treasury Bills to bridge budget deficits.View Answer Freed the central bank's monetary policy from direct fiscal pressure of the executive and permanently ended the uncontrolled automatic monetization of the fiscal deficit.
Ways and Means Advances (WMA Framework)View Answer 90-day short-term credit from the RBI to bridge temporary cash mismatches between the government's daily tax revenue inflows and expenditure outflows.View Answer Provided the government with safe liquidity for day-to-day expenses without permanent monetization of the deficit; included penal interest rates for exceeding time or amount limits.
Four Mandatory FRBM StatementsView Answer Presenting the Macroeconomic Framework Statement, Medium-Term Fiscal Policy Statement, Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement, and Medium-Term Expenditure Framework in Parliament alongside the Budget.View Answer Historic improvement in executive financial transparency; enhanced credibility of budget estimates through three-year rolling targets.
Elimination of Off-Budget BorrowingView Answer Bringing borrowings raised by PSUs such as FCI from the National Small Savings Fund (NSSF) or banks for government subsidies directly onto the budget.View Answer Ended the 'hidden' fiscal deficit in budgetary accounts; ensured authentic disclosure of actual sovereign liabilities before international credit rating agencies and Parliament.
Escape Clause (Section 4(2))View Answer Permits a 0.5% deviation from the deficit target during war, national security crises, agricultural distress, sweeping structural reforms, or a decline of over 1% in Gross Value Added (GVA).View Answer Provided necessary policy flexibility for public welfare and economic stimulus instead of fiscal rigidity during unprecedented economic crises (such as the COVID-19 pandemic).

One-Liner Questions

What is the full official name of the FRBM Act, and in which year was it passed by Parliament?
Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2003.

From which date was the FRBM Act formally notified and enforced in India?
From 5 July 2004 (along with the FRBM Rules, 2004).
Tap to view answer
During the tenure of which Finance Minister was the FRBM Act first introduced as a Bill in Parliament?
In December 2000, by the then Finance Minister Yashwant Sinha (in the Atal Bihari Vajpayee government).
Tap to view answer
Who was the chairman of the high-level committee constituted in 2000 to draft the FRBM Bill?
Dr. E. A. S. Sarma (then Secretary, Department of Economic Affairs).
Tap to view answer
What was the primary and central legislative objective of the FRBM Act, 2003?
To establish fiscal discipline in public financial management, eliminate/limit the fiscal and revenue deficits, and ensure inter-generational equity.
Tap to view answer
What is meant by 'Inter-Generational Equity' in the original framework of the FRBM Act?
Not shifting the burden of the present generation's consumption and daily administrative expenses onto future generations by accumulating heavy public debt.
Tap to view answer
According to the original FRBM Act, 2003 and its rules, to what level was the 'Fiscal Deficit' targeted to be limited?
Bringing the fiscal deficit down to 3% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) by 31 March 2008 (later extended to 2009).
Tap to view answer
What mandatory legal target was set regarding the 'Revenue Deficit' under the original FRBM law?
Reducing the revenue deficit to zero (0%) by 31 March 2008—that is, achieving complete balance or surplus on the revenue account.
Tap to view answer
According to the FRBM Rules, by what minimum percentage points per year were the fiscal deficit and revenue deficit targeted to be reduced?
Reducing the fiscal deficit by at least 0.3% of GDP per year and the revenue deficit by at least 0.5% per year.
Tap to view answer
Under the FRBM Act, by how much were the Central Government's 'Total Liabilities' targeted to be reduced annually?
A gradual annual reduction of 1% of GDP in total liabilities starting from FY 2004-05.
Tap to view answer
What limit did the FRBM law set on sovereign loan guarantees given by the Central Government?
New guarantees given in any financial year cannot exceed 0.5% of the estimated GDP for that year.
Tap to view answer
In FY 2007-08, to what historic low percentage of GDP had India's fiscal deficit declined?
To just 2.54% of GDP (the FRBM target of 3% was achieved ahead of schedule).
Tap to view answer
Due to which global event did the implementation of FRBM targets have to be abruptly suspended in 2008?
Due to the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, to counter which the government rolled out massive fiscal stimulus packages.
Tap to view answer
After the 2008 crisis, to what percentage of GDP did India's fiscal deficit jump in 2008-09 and 2009-10?
To over 6% to 6.5% of GDP.
Tap to view answer
Which new intermediate deficit indicator was added to the FRBM law through the Finance Act in 2012?
'Effective Revenue Deficit' (ERD), with a new target set to reduce it to zero.
Tap to view answer
Which committee was constituted by the Central Government in May 2016 for a comprehensive review of the FRBM law?
The N. K. Singh Committee (FRBM Review Committee).
Tap to view answer
Which then-Governor of the Reserve Bank of India was included as a member of the N. K. Singh Committee?
Dr. Urjit Patel (Chief Economic Adviser Dr. Arvind Subramanian and Sumit Bose were also members).
Tap to view answer
In which year did the N. K. Singh Committee submit its landmark report to the government?
In January 2017 (report titled: 'Responsible Growth: A Fiscal Framework for 21st Century India').
Tap to view answer
Which key macroeconomic variable did the N. K. Singh Committee recommend making the primary anchor of fiscal policy instead of the annual fiscal deficit?
The 'Debt-to-GDP Ratio'.
Tap to view answer
What target for general government debt (combined Centre + States) by 2023 was recommended by the N. K. Singh Committee?
60% of GDP (in which Central Government debt should be 40% and combined debt of all States should be 20%).
Tap to view answer
To what level did the N. K. Singh Committee recommend bringing the Central Government's fiscal deficit by FY 2022-23?
To 2.5% of GDP (while recommending maintaining it at 3.0% from 2017-18 to 2019-20).
Tap to view answer
What specific numerical recommendation did the N. K. Singh Committee make regarding the 'Revenue Deficit'?
Bringing the revenue deficit down to 0.8% of GDP by 2022-23 (and advising complete abolition of the misleading concept of 'Effective Revenue Deficit').
Tap to view answer
Which new institutional body did the N. K. Singh Committee suggest setting up for independent budgetary oversight?
An autonomous 'Fiscal Council' to conduct independent evaluations of budget estimates.
Tap to view answer
What is meant by the 'Escape Clause' under Section 4(2) of the FRBM Act?
Legal leeway for the Central Government to deviate by up to 0.5% (50 basis points) from the prescribed annual fiscal deficit target under extraordinary and adverse circumstances.
Tap to view answer
According to the N. K. Singh Committee, on what specific grounds can the government invoke the 'Escape Clause'?
1. National security and war, 2. Severe natural calamity, 3. Severe distress in the agricultural sector, 4. Far-reaching structural reforms (such as GST), and 5. A decline of at least 3% in real GVA over four consecutive quarters.
Tap to view answer
In contrast to the Escape Clause in the FRBM law, what does the 'Buoyancy Clause' state?
When the economy records unexpectedly rapid growth and real GVA grows by 3% above normal, it will be mandatory for the government to reduce the deficit target by an additional 0.5%.
Tap to view answer
Which major recommendations of the N. K. Singh Committee were incorporated into the FRBM law through the Finance Act, 2018?
The target of bringing general government debt to 60% and Central debt to 40% by 2024-25, and giving statutory form to the new Escape Clause under Section 4(2).
Tap to view answer
During the COVID-19 pandemic (2020-21), to what percentage of GDP did the Government of India's fiscal deficit rise?
To 9.2% of GDP (the highest deficit in the history of independent India).
Tap to view answer
What new medium-term fiscal consolidation target did the government announce in the Union Budget 2021-22?
Bringing the fiscal deficit below 4.5% of GDP by FY 2025-26.
Tap to view answer
Do all states in India have their own FRBM laws?
Yes; on the recommendation of the 12th Finance Commission, all 28 states in India enacted their own State FRBM Acts, under which the normal fiscal deficit of states is capped at 3% of GSDP.
Tap to view answer
What is the accurate and comprehensive concept of 'Fiscal Discipline' in economics?
The government strictly confining its public expenditure within the limits of its available revenue and non-inflationary sustainable borrowings.
Tap to view answer
What is the immediate impact of fiscal indiscipline or 'Fiscal Profligacy' on a country's macroeconomy?
Explosive growth in public debt, a sharp surge in domestic inflation, and widening of the Current Account Deficit (CAD) to dangerous levels.
Tap to view answer
What is the direct relationship between fiscal discipline and 'Monetary Stability'?
If the government runs an uncontrolled fiscal deficit, it becomes impossible for the central bank (RBI) to control inflation and keep interest rates stable.
Tap to view answer
What does the 'Twin Deficit Hypothesis' explain in economics?
The direct positive correlation between a high Fiscal Deficit and a high Current Account Deficit (CAD).
Tap to view answer
How does an increase in the fiscal deficit widen the Current Account Deficit (CAD)?
Rising government spending lowers national savings and increases demand for imports, which worsens the trade balance and makes the country dependent on foreign borrowing.
Tap to view answer
What is the definition of 'Debt Sustainability' in the context of public debt?
A financial condition where the government is able to service its existing and future debt obligations (principal and interest) without defaulting, without debt restructuring, and without halting economic growth.
Tap to view answer
What is the mathematical equation for the 'Domar Debt Stability Condition'?
g > r (the nominal growth rate 'g' of the economy must exceed the nominal interest rate 'r' paid by the government).
Tap to view answer
If a country's economic growth rate exceeds the interest rate (g > r), what is the impact on public debt?
The debt-to-GDP ratio declines naturally over time; the government does not have to undertake overly harsh austerity to repay debt.
Tap to view answer
What situation will arise if the interest rate exceeds the growth rate (r > g) and the government's primary deficit also persists?
Public debt will get trapped in a vicious cycle, grow uncontrollably, and push the country to the brink of sovereign bankruptcy.
Tap to view answer
What positive impact does fiscal discipline have on 'Private Corporate Investment'?
Keeping government borrowing limited preserves liquidity in banks, keeps interest rates low, and makes cheap credit accessible to private enterprises (crowding-in).
Tap to view answer
What is the importance of fiscal discipline from the perspective of international credit rating agencies (Moody's, S&P, Fitch)?
It strengthens the country's Sovereign Credit Rating, enabling Indian companies and the government to access foreign capital from international markets at much cheaper rates.
Tap to view answer
What are the two main pathways for 'Fiscal Consolidation'?
1. Revenue-led consolidation (increasing revenue by widening the tax base), and 2. Expenditure-led consolidation (reducing unproductive and wasteful spending).
Tap to view answer
What is considered 'Quality Fiscal Adjustment' for achieving fiscal discipline?
Reducing the deficit solely through cuts in unproductive revenue expenditure and unwanted outlays without slashing capital expenditure (roads, railways, schools, hospitals).
Tap to view answer
Why do economists consider it harmful if the government drastically cuts its 'Capital Expenditure' (Capex) to meet the fiscal deficit target?
Because it severely cripples the country's future productive capacity, infrastructure, and long-term GDP growth rate.
Tap to view answer
What is the standard definition of 'Deficit Financing' in fiscal policy?
The various financial methods adopted by the government to bridge the financial gap (fiscal deficit) between its total expenditure and its regular current income.
Tap to view answer
In the early decades of independent India, what was the main and traditional meaning attributed to 'Deficit Financing'?
Covering the government's budget deficit by having the central bank (RBI) directly print new currency notes (Monetized Deficit).
Tap to view answer
What are the two main and non-inflationary sources of deficit financing in modern macroeconomics?
1. Internal Market Borrowings, and 2. External Debt.
Tap to view answer
What were 'Ad-hoc Treasury Bills' (91-day) that were used in deficit financing?
Bills issued by the government at a fixed nominal concessional rate of 4.6%, which the RBI automatically purchased by directly printing new notes.
Tap to view answer
What was the most damaging economic distortion of financing deficits through Ad-hoc Treasury Bills?
Uncontrolled and excessive expansion of money supply (M3) in the economy, resulting in severe double-digit inflation during the 1970s and 1980s.
Tap to view answer
When was the historic agreement signed in India to end the direct automatic monetization of deficits through Ad-hoc Treasury Bills?
On 9 September 1994 between RBI Governor Dr. C. Rangarajan and the Union Ministry of Finance, taking full effect from 31 March 1997.
Tap to view answer
Which new system was introduced from 1 April 1997 in place of direct automatic monetization of deficits?
The 'Ways and Means Advances' (WMA) system.
Tap to view answer
What is the basic nature of Ways and Means Advances (WMA)?
It is not permanent debt to cover the budget deficit, but purely a short-term credit facility to bridge temporary cash mismatches between tax revenue inflows and daily government spending.
Tap to view answer
What is the maximum duration of short-term advances given to the Central Government under WMA?
Maximum 90 days (3 months).
Tap to view answer
At what rate does the RBI charge interest from the government on loans taken within the WMA limit?
At the policy Repo Rate.
Tap to view answer
What is the situation called if the government withdraws an amount exceeding the sanctioned WMA limit?
'Overdraft' (OD).
Tap to view answer
At what interest rate does the RBI charge penal interest from the Central Government in the event of an Overdraft (OD)?
At a high penal rate of Repo Rate + 2%.
Tap to view answer
For how many consecutive working days can the Central Government remain in overdraft at a stretch?
For a maximum of 10 consecutive working days (beyond which the RBI is legally authorized to stop payments).
Tap to view answer
Do State Governments also receive the WMA facility from the RBI?
Yes; State Governments receive two types of WMA: 1. Special WMA (SDF - against state securities), and 2. Normal WMA (unsecured credit).
Tap to view answer
Which committee was constituted by the RBI to periodically review WMA and overdraft rules for State Governments?
The Sudhir Shrivastava Committee (2020-21).
Tap to view answer
What historic statutory restriction did Section 5 of the FRBM Act, 2003 place on deficit financing?
From 1 April 2006, it completely prohibited the RBI from directly participating in the primary market issues of government securities.
Tap to view answer
After Section 5 of the FRBM Act came into force, from where does the government have to borrow to bridge its fiscal deficit?
From the open financial market (commercial banks, insurance companies, mutual funds, and primary dealers) at competitive market rates.
Tap to view answer
Can the RBI currently purchase government securities from the secondary market?
Yes; it can purchase them from the secondary market under 'Open Market Operations' (OMOs) solely for liquidity management and monetary policy operations, not for directly lending to the government.
Tap to view answer
Which is currently the largest domestic source for bridging the Government of India's fiscal deficit?
Market borrowings through Dated Government Securities (Dated G-Secs) and Treasury Bills (T-Bills) (accounting for about 70% to 75% share).
Tap to view answer
Apart from market borrowings, which is the second largest domestic source for financing the fiscal deficit?
The National Small Savings Fund (NSSF - net deposits received from post office savings, PPF, Sukanya Samriddhi, etc.).
Tap to view answer
What is the share of 'External Debt' in the total fiscal deficit of the Government of India?
Extremely low; only about 2% to 4% of total deficit financing (India raises most of its sovereign borrowing in its domestic currency, the rupee).
Tap to view answer
What is the biggest protective advantage to the Indian economy of having most of its sovereign borrowing in domestic currency (rupees)?
India remains completely insulated from foreign currency depreciation risk and 1997 East Asian-style external sovereign debt crises.
Tap to view answer
What is the modern meaning of 'Monetization of Deficit' as a form of deficit financing?
When the central bank creates new money by directly buying government bonds or purchasing them in large quantities from the secondary market.
Tap to view answer
Did the Government of India opt for direct monetization of the deficit during the COVID-19 pandemic?
No; the Government of India and the RBI refrained from the temptation of directly printing notes and relied on market borrowings and transparent secondary liquidity tools like G-SAP.
Tap to view answer
How does uncontrolled deficit financing in developing countries take the form of an 'Inflation Tax'?
Excessive money printing erodes the purchasing power of currency, thereby depleting the real wealth of poor and fixed-income sections of society, acting like an indirect hidden tax.
Tap to view answer
When does deficit financing prove 'beneficial' for economic growth?
When every single rupee borrowed is used to build high-quality productive capital assets (Capex) whose economic return exceeds the interest rate on the borrowing.
Tap to view answer
When does deficit financing become 'destructive' for the economy?
When deficit funds are squandered on salaries, unproductive subsidies, administrative consumption, and paying interest on old loans.
Tap to view answer
What is meant by 'Fiscal Transparency' in fiscal policy?
Full, accurate, timely, and impartial disclosure by the government before Parliament and the public of all its revenues, expenditures, assets, public debts, and future contingent liabilities.
Tap to view answer
Which mandatory documents does the Central Government have to present in Parliament along with the Budget under Section 3 of the FRBM Act, 2003?
Four specific Fiscal Policy Statements.
Tap to view answer
Which are those four mandatory budgetary documents presented under the FRBM Act?
1. Macroeconomic Framework Statement, 2. Medium-Term Fiscal Policy Statement, 3. Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement, and 4. Medium-Term Expenditure Framework Statement.
Tap to view answer
What is presented in the 'Macroeconomic Framework Statement'?
A comprehensive assessment of overall economic growth prospects, real GDP growth rate, current account balance, inflation, and fundamental external sector indicators.
Tap to view answer
What is the function of the 'Medium-Term Fiscal Policy Statement'?
Setting specific numerical three-year rolling targets for the fiscal deficit, revenue deficit, primary deficit, tax revenue, and total debt for the next three years.
Tap to view answer
What is included in the 'Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement'?
An outline of the government's overall strategic priorities and policy rationale regarding taxation, public expenditure, borrowing, and non-tax revenue for the current year.
Tap to view answer
In which session is the 'Medium-Term Expenditure Framework Statement' (MTEF) presented?
In the Monsoon Session of Parliament after the Budget Session; it provides a detailed three-year allocation blueprint of capital and revenue expenditure for various Union Ministries.

What is the rule regarding the 'Quarterly Review' of government revenue and expenditure under the FRBM Act?
At the end of every quarter of each financial year, the Finance Minister is required to lay a review report on budget trends and deficit progress before both Houses of Parliament.

What are 'Off-Budget Borrowings' in the context of fiscal transparency?
Making Public Sector Undertakings (FCI, etc.) take loans to pay for government expenses (such as food subsidy, fertilizer subsidy) instead of providing funds directly from the budget, so that the official fiscal deficit appears lower in the budget.
Tap to view answer
Which public institution was historically the largest and primary vehicle for off-budget borrowing in India?
The Food Corporation of India (FCI), which was forced to borrow lakhs of crores of rupees from the National Small Savings Fund (NSSF) to meet food subsidy arrears.
Tap to view answer
What sharp remark did the Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG) make on off-budget borrowing in its 2019 audit report?
That off-budget borrowing severely undermines budget transparency and the government's actual fiscal deficit is much higher than the figure shown in the budget.
Tap to view answer
What bold step did Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman take in the Union Budget 2021-22 to end off-budget borrowing?
She subsumed all past loans taken by FCI from the NSSF directly into the official expenditure of the Union Budget and ensured 100% budgetary payment for food subsidies going forward.
Tap to view answer
What immediate impact was seen on the official fiscal deficit after completely ending off-budget borrowing and bringing it onto the budget?
The official fiscal deficit figure became transparent and rose higher, but the international credibility, authenticity, and transparency of the budget saw a historic improvement.
Tap to view answer
What is the role of the 'Guarantee Redemption Fund' (GRF) in the budget for fiscal transparency?
A special fund maintained with the RBI used to meet liabilities in case of default on loan guarantees extended by the government to public sector undertakings.

Which hidden liabilities are mandated to be disclosed under the 'Receipt Budget' in budget documents?
Total outstanding amount of government guarantees, liabilities of externally aided projects, and the government's Contingent Liabilities.

1. Fiscal Reporting, 2. Fiscal Forecasting and Budgeting, 3. Fiscal Risk Analysis and Management, and 4. Resource Revenue Management.

It stopped unspent funds of Centrally Sponsored Schemes from lying idle in state bank accounts and drastically reduced the government's borrowing costs.

Among fiscal deficit, primary deficit, and revenue deficit, which deficit's reduction to zero is considered most desirable in a welfare-oriented developing country?

Is having a 0% fiscal deficit in a developing country always considered optimal from an economic perspective?

What is the final and comprehensive macroeconomic conclusion of studying Fiscal Policy Part 2?
The FRBM Act and the 1997 WMA reform are the two strongest pillars of modern fiscal discipline in India; this framework proves that market-based deficit financing free from the temptation of printing notes, strict budgetary transparency, complete elimination of off-budget borrowing, and prudent capital expenditure are the core mantras for India's economic sovereignty and long-term sustainable growth.
