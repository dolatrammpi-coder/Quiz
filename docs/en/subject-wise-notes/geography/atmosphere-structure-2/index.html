Structure of the Atmosphere - Part 2 (Atmosphere Structure 2)

Mesosphere, Ionosphere, Exosphere, Reflection of Radio Waves, Aurora (Polar Lights), and Upper Atmospheric Processes

🔍
Quick Reference
Important Facts & Contributions
Atmosphere Structure 2
Layer / PhenomenonKey CharacteristicAssociated Impact
Mesosphere
View Answer
Extends from the Stratopause (50 km) up to 80 km altitude; sharp drop in temperature with height; coldest layer of the atmosphere
View Answer
Most meteors entering from space at high velocity burn up and disintegrate in this layer due to air friction
Mesopause
View Answer
Upper boundary of the Mesosphere (~80 to 85 km); temperature drops to -80°C to -100°C (-130°F)
View Answer
Thermal transition boundary between the Mesosphere and Thermosphere; site of the lowest recorded natural atmospheric temperature
Noctilucent Clouds
View Answer
Luminous bluish-white clouds formed near the Mesopause (80–85 km) by tiny ice crystals nucleating on meteoric dust particles
View Answer
Visible glowing high in the night sky after sunset at high latitudes (50°–70°) during summer
Kármán Line
View Answer
Imaginary boundary at exactly 100 km (62 miles) above sea level recognized by the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale (FAI)
View Answer
Legal and physical dividing line between the Earth's atmosphere and Outer Space
Thermosphere
View Answer
Extends from 80 km to 640 km; rapid rise in kinetic temperature with altitude (1,200°C+) due to absorption of solar UV and X-rays
View Answer
Due to extremely rarefied air density, high kinetic temperature does not impart sensible heat to human skin or spacecraft

Important Facts & Contributions
Atmosphere Structure 2
Layer / PhenomenonKey CharacteristicAssociated Impact
D-Layer (80–99 km)
View Answer
Lowest ionospheric layer; formed only during daytime by solar UV radiation and disappears completely at night
View Answer
Reflects low-frequency (LF/VLF) radio waves while absorbing medium- and high-frequency waves
E-Layer (Kennelly–Heaviside Layer)
View Answer
99 to 130–150 km altitude; permanent ionized layer formed by ionization of nitrogen and oxygen molecules
View Answer
Reflects medium-frequency (MF) radio waves; primary reflector for terrestrial AM radio broadcasting
F-Layer (Appleton–Barnett Layer)
View Answer
150 to 400 km; splits into F1 and F2 sub-layers during daytime and merges into a single dense F-layer at night
View Answer
Strongly reflects high-frequency (HF / Shortwave) radio waves; backbone of long-distance global wireless communication
Exosphere
View Answer
Outermost fringe of the atmosphere extending from 640 km to 10,000 km; dominated by extremely rarefied Helium and Hydrogen
View Answer
Continuous escape of light gas molecules into space; orbital zone of artificial satellites
Aurora (Polar Lights)
View Answer
Luminescent glow produced when solar wind charged particles (electrons/protons) collide with oxygen and nitrogen atoms in the ionosphere
View Answer
Spectacular displays of 'Aurora Borealis' (green/red glow) over the North Pole and 'Aurora Australis' over the South Pole
Van Allen Radiation Belts
View Answer
Two giant concentric torus-shaped belts of energetic charged particles trapped in space by Earth's Magnetosphere
View Answer
Magnetic shield protecting Earth's biosphere and low-orbit satellites from lethal solar flares and cosmic radiation

How does temperature change with increasing altitude in the Thermosphere?

What is the main physical cause of this extreme rise in temperature in the Thermosphere?

Despite temperatures exceeding 1,500°C in the Thermosphere, why would an astronaut not feel hot there?
Because the air density is so extremely low that there are too few gas molecule collisions to transfer significant thermal energy

Why does the D-Layer exist only during daytime and vanish completely at night?
