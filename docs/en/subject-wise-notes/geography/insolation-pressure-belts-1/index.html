Insolation and Pressure Belts - Part 1 (Insolation Pressure Belts 1)

Insolation, Heat Budget of the Earth, Horizontal & Vertical Distribution of Temperature, and Inversion of Temperature

View Answer

Important Facts & Contributions
Insolation Pressure Belts 1
Zone / PhenomenonLocation / ConditionKey Characteristic
Heat Surplus Zone
View Answer
Tropical and subtropical belt between 40° North and 40° South latitudes
View Answer
Incoming solar radiation exceeds outgoing terrestrial radiation; acts as the primary thermal engine driving global winds and ocean currents
Heat Deficit Zone
View Answer
High-latitude and polar belts from 40° latitudes to both Poles (90° N & S)
View Answer
Outgoing terrestrial radiation exceeds incoming insolation; receives poleward heat transfer from lower latitudes to prevent progressive freezing
Normal Lapse Rate
View Answer
Vertical profile in the Troposphere (from surface to Tropopause)
View Answer
Natural decrease in air temperature with altitude at an average rate of 1°C per 165 metres or 6.5°C per 1,000 metres (1 km)
Surface / Radiation Inversion
View Answer
Directly above snow-covered or flat land surfaces on clear, calm winter nights
View Answer
Rapid nocturnal terrestrial radiation cools the ground; lower air becomes cold and dense beneath warmer air aloft
Valley Inversion (Drainage Inversion)
View Answer
In mountain valleys at night (along the valley floor)
View Answer
Cold, dense katabatic winds drain downslope under gravity to pool on the valley floor, causing frost below while middle slopes remain warmer
Thermal Anomaly
View Answer
Across different locations along the same parallel of latitude
View Answer
Mathematical difference between the actual mean temperature of a place and the average temperature of its latitude circle

One-Liner Questions

What is the solar radiation energy intercepted by the Earth's surface and its atmosphere technically called?
Insolation (Incoming Solar Radiation)
Tap to view answer
In what physical wave form does energy radiated from the Sun reach the Earth?
In the form of Shortwave Electromagnetic Waves
Tap to view answer
What minute fraction of the total energy radiated by the Sun is intercepted by the Earth?
Approximately one two-billionth part (1 / 2,000,000,000th part)
Tap to view answer
What is the average rate at which solar energy is received per sq cm per minute at the outer limit of the atmosphere called?
Solar Constant
Tap to view answer
What is the standard scientific value of the Solar Constant?
Approximately 1.94 to 2.0 calories per sq cm per minute (~1,361 Watts/sq metre)
Tap to view answer
Which is the primary geographical factor controlling the amount and intensity of insolation received on the Earth's surface?
Angle of Inclination (Angle of Incidence) of the Sun's rays
Tap to view answer
Why does a higher angle of incidence (vertical rays) deliver greater insolation per unit area?
Because vertical rays are concentrated over a smaller surface area and pass through a shorter, thinner path of the atmosphere
Tap to view answer
What are the two main reasons why oblique (slanting) rays deliver less insolation?
The energy is spread over a much wider surface area and suffers greater scattering, reflection, and absorption while traversing a thicker atmospheric path
Tap to view answer
How does the amount of insolation received change as one moves from the Equator towards the Poles?
The amount of insolation decreases progressively towards the Poles
Tap to view answer
The annual insolation received at the Poles is approximately what percentage of the insolation received at the Equator?
Approximately 40 percent (40%)
Tap to view answer
Which is the second major astronomical factor determining the daily amount of insolation received at a place?
Duration of Daylight (Length of the Day)
Tap to view answer
During the Summer Solstice on 21 June in the Northern Hemisphere, where are the longest day and maximum daily insolation recorded?
Along the Tropic of Cancer (23.5° N) and the North Polar region (at the top of the atmosphere)
Tap to view answer
During the Winter Solstice on 22 December, where is maximum insolation received in the Southern Hemisphere?
Along the Tropic of Capricorn (23.5° S) and the Antarctic polar region
Tap to view answer
What is the astronomical position on 21 March and 23 September when day and night are equal (12 hours each) across the entire Earth called?
Equinox

When the Earth is closest to the Sun (~147 million km) on 3 January, what is that orbital position called?
Perihelion

When the Earth is farthest from the Sun (~152 million km) on 4 July, what is that orbital position called?
Aphelion
Tap to view answer
How much more insolation (in percentage) does the Earth receive at Perihelion (3 January) compared to Aphelion (4 July)?
Approximately 7 percent (7%) more insolation
Tap to view answer
Despite the Earth receiving 7% more solar energy in January, why does the Northern Hemisphere experience winter in January?
Because the Northern Hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun, making solar rays oblique and daylight hours short
Tap to view answer
Why is the highest annual surface insolation received over subtropical deserts rather than directly over the Equator?
Because subtropical deserts have clear, cloudless skies and dry air, whereas the Equator has heavy afternoon cloud cover
Tap to view answer
Why does the Equatorial zone not record the highest surface insolation despite receiving nearly vertical sunbeams year-round?
Due to persistent afternoon convective cloudiness (cumulonimbus clouds) that reflects a large share of sunlight

What is the direction in which a mountain slope faces with respect to incoming sunlight—affecting local insolation—called?
Aspect of Slope
Tap to view answer
In the Northern Hemisphere (such as the Himalayas and Alps), which mountain slopes receive more sunlight and have denser human settlements and vegetation?
South-facing slopes (Adret / sunny slopes)
Tap to view answer
What is the deflection and dispersal of incoming solar radiation in all directions by fine dust particles and gas molecules called?
Scattering
Tap to view answer
What is the indirect sunlight that reaches the Earth's surface from the blue sky and clouds due to scattering and reflection called?
Diffuse Daylight (Skylight / Diffuse Solar Radiation)
Tap to view answer
What is the percentage ratio of incoming solar radiation reflected directly back by a surface without being absorbed called?
Albedo (Reflectivity)
Tap to view answer
Among natural surfaces on Earth, which surface has the highest Albedo (80% to 90%)?
Freshly fallen clean Snow
Tap to view answer
What is the typical Albedo of dense dark forest canopies and wet black ploughed soil?
Very low (approximately 5 to 15 percent)
Tap to view answer
What is the overall average Planetary Albedo of the Earth and its atmosphere as a whole?
Approximately 35 percent (or 0.35)
Tap to view answer
What is the exact breakdown of the 35 units of solar radiation reflected as Earth's Planetary Albedo?
27 units reflected from cloud tops, 2 units from snow and ice-covered surfaces, and 6 units scattered back into space by atmospheric dust/air
Tap to view answer
What is the equilibrium balance between total incoming solar radiation absorbed by the Earth-atmosphere system and total outgoing radiation returned to space called?
Heat Budget of the Earth
Tap to view answer
Assuming 100 units of incoming solar radiation at the top of the atmosphere, how many units are actually absorbed by the Earth's surface?
A total of 51 units (17 units from direct solar radiation + 34 units from diffuse daylight)
Tap to view answer
Out of the 100 incoming solar units, how many units are directly absorbed by the atmosphere during downward passage?
14 units (absorbed by stratospheric ozone, water vapour, and dust)
Tap to view answer
Out of the 51 units radiated back by the Earth's surface as terrestrial radiation, how many units escape directly into space through the 'Atmospheric Window'?
17 units (escaping directly into space without heating the atmosphere)
Tap to view answer
How are the remaining 34 units of outgoing surface heat absorbed by the atmosphere?
6 units by direct radiation absorption, 9 units via convection and turbulence, and 19 units via latent heat of condensation
Tap to view answer
How many total units of heat does the atmosphere absorb from both solar and terrestrial sources combined?
48 units (14 units from incoming solar radiation + 34 units from outgoing terrestrial radiation)
Tap to view answer
Where does the atmosphere ultimately radiate all 48 units of heat that it absorbs?
Back into outer space (48 units)
Tap to view answer
Which two components add up to the 65 units of longwave radiation returned to space, balancing the 65 solar units absorbed (100 - 35 albedo)?
17 units radiated directly from the Earth's surface + 48 units radiated by the atmosphere = 65 units
Tap to view answer
After being heated by shortwave sunlight, in what wavelength band does the Earth's surface radiate heat back?
In the form of Longwave Infrared Terrestrial Radiation
Tap to view answer
Why is the atmosphere largely transparent to incoming solar radiation but opaque to outgoing terrestrial radiation?
Because greenhouse gases like water vapour and CO₂ allow shortwave solar rays to pass through but strongly absorb longwave infrared rays
Tap to view answer
Why is the Troposphere heated from the bottom upwards rather than from the top downwards?
Because it absorbs very little direct sunlight (only 14 units) and is primarily heated by longwave terrestrial radiation, conduction, convection, and latent heat from the ground
Tap to view answer
Within which latitudinal belt does incoming insolation exceed outgoing terrestrial radiation (Heat Surplus Zone)?
Between 40° North and 40° South latitudes (the tropical and subtropical belt)
Tap to view answer
In which latitudinal belts does outgoing terrestrial radiation exceed incoming insolation (Heat Deficit Zones)?
From 40° latitudes to the Poles (90° N and S) in both hemispheres
Tap to view answer
Why does the tropical heat-surplus zone not become progressively hotter and the polar deficit zone not freeze progressively colder each year?
Because planetary winds (~75%) and ocean currents (~25%) continuously transport surplus heat from the tropics toward the poles
Tap to view answer
What is the transfer of heat through molecular contact between the warm ground surface and the thin layer of air touching it called?
Conduction (effective only in the lowest boundary layer touching the ground)
Tap to view answer
What is the vertical circulation process in which air heated at the surface expands, rises as thermals, and cooler air sinks called?
Convection (the primary mechanism of vertical heat transfer in the troposphere)
Tap to view answer
What is the horizontal transfer of heat from one region to another by the horizontal movement of wind called?
Advection
Tap to view answer
The hot, dry summer wind 'Loo' blowing across the plains of northern India is a classic result of which heat transfer process?
Advection
Tap to view answer
What is the hidden thermal energy absorbed during evaporation of water and released into the atmosphere during cloud condensation called?
Latent Heat of Condensation (and Vaporization)
Tap to view answer
What are imaginary lines drawn on a weather map connecting places having the same mean temperature (reduced to sea level) called?
Isotherms
Tap to view answer
When drawing world Isotherm maps, how are station temperatures recorded at different elevations standardized?
They are reduced to Sea Level (by adding 6.5°C per km of elevation) to eliminate the local effect of altitude
Tap to view answer
Roughly parallel to which lines on the globe do Isotherms generally run?
Parallel to lines of Latitude (in an East–West direction)
Tap to view answer
Why are Isotherms much straighter, more regular, and more closely parallel to latitudes in the Southern Hemisphere than in the Northern Hemisphere?
Because the Southern Hemisphere is overwhelmingly covered by continuous oceans (~81% water) with very little landmass
Tap to view answer
In the Northern Hemisphere in January (winter), in which direction do Isotherms bend when passing from cold continents over to warmer oceans?
Poleward (towards the North, because oceans remain warmer than land in winter)
Tap to view answer
In the Northern Hemisphere in July (summer), in which direction do Isotherms bend when passing from cooler oceans onto hot continents?
Poleward (towards the North, because continental interiors heat up much more than oceans in summer)
Tap to view answer
What is the climatic effect experienced in continental interiors—characterized by extreme summer heat, severe winter cold, and high annual temperature range due to distance from the sea—called?
Continentality (Continental Climate)
Tap to view answer
What causes coastal regions near oceans to experience equable, moderate temperatures with a low temperature range year-round?
Maritime Influence (due to the high specific heat and mixing of ocean water)
Tap to view answer
What are the three main physical reasons why water heats up and cools down much more slowly than land?
High specific heat of water (~2.5 to 5 times higher than rock), deep penetration of solar rays in transparent water, and continuous vertical/horizontal mixing

What is the numerical difference between the highest and lowest temperatures recorded over a given period called?
Range of Temperature

What is the difference between the maximum daytime temperature (usually 2–4 PM) and minimum nighttime temperature (just before sunrise) called?
Diurnal Range of Temperature
Tap to view answer
What is the difference between the mean temperature of the warmest month and the coldest month of the year called?
Annual Range of Temperature

Where in the world is the highest Annual Range of Temperature (>60°C) recorded?
In Verkhoyansk and Oymyakon (Siberia, Russia — annual range of ~65°C to 68°C)
Tap to view answer
In which geographical regions is the lowest Annual Range of Temperature (only 2°C to 3°C) found?
In Equatorial and maritime oceanic regions

What is the difference between the actual mean temperature of a place and the average temperature of its entire parallel of latitude called?
Thermal Anomaly
Tap to view answer
If the mean temperature of a place is higher than the average temperature of its latitude, what is it called?
Positive Thermal Anomaly (such as Western Europe warmed by the North Atlantic Drift / Gulf Stream)
Tap to view answer
If the mean temperature of a place is lower than the average temperature of its latitude, what is it called?
Negative Thermal Anomaly (such as continental interiors in winter or coasts washed by the cold Labrador Current)
Tap to view answer
Where in the world is the largest Positive Thermal Anomaly recorded during winter?
Over the North-Eastern Atlantic Ocean along the Norwegian coast (more than +20°C warmer than the latitudinal average)
Tap to view answer
What is the natural rate of decrease of temperature with increasing altitude in the Troposphere called?
Normal Lapse Rate
Tap to view answer
What is the standard numerical value of the Normal Lapse Rate?
6.5°C per 1,000 metres (1 km) or 1°C per 165 metres
Tap to view answer
Why are mountain hill stations (such as Shimla or Darjeeling) much cooler than plains cities at the same latitude (such as Jalandhar or Siliguri)?
Due to the Normal Lapse Rate and the thin, clean, less heat-retaining air at higher altitudes

When temperature in the troposphere increases with increasing altitude instead of decreasing (warm air overlying cold air), what is the condition called?
Inversion of Temperature (Thermal Inversion)
Tap to view answer
How does an Inversion of Temperature affect vertical atmospheric stability?
It makes the lower atmosphere extremely stable and completely suppresses vertical convection
Tap to view answer
Which four ideal meteorological conditions are required for the development of a surface Radiation Inversion?
Long winter nights, clear cloudless sky, calm or very light wind, and dry air / snow-covered surface
Tap to view answer
How do long winter nights promote surface temperature inversion?
Because outgoing nocturnal terrestrial radiation continues for many hours, cooling the ground far below the temperature of the air above
Tap to view answer
Why is a clear, cloudless sky essential for radiation temperature inversion?
Because clouds trap outgoing infrared radiation like a blanket; a clear sky allows heat to escape rapidly into space

What are the cold, dense winds that cool by radiation on upper mountain slopes at night and drain down into the valley floor under gravity called?

What is the specific temperature inversion formed in mountain valleys by downslope drainage of cold air at night called?
Valley Inversion (or Air-Drainage / Topographic Inversion)

Due to Valley Inversion, why are human settlements, hotels, and fruit orchards (apple/coffee) in mountain valleys located on middle slopes ('Thermal Belt') rather than on the valley floor?
Because cold air pools on the valley floor causing severe frost damage to crops, whereas middle slopes remain warmer and frost-free

Why does ground-level air pollution reach hazardous levels during a temperature inversion?
Because dense cold air is trapped near the ground and the overlying warm inversion layer acts like a lid preventing upward dispersion of pollutants

What is the general thermal relationship between air temperature and atmospheric pressure?
