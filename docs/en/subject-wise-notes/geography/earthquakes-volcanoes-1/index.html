Earthquakes and Volcanoes - Part 1 (Earthquakes Volcanoes 1)

Origin of Earthquakes (Focus & Epicentre), Elastic Rebound Theory, Seismic Waves (P, S, L), Shadow Zones, and Global Distribution

🔍
Quick Reference
Important Facts & Contributions
Earthquakes Volcanoes 1
Seismic ElementKey CharacteristicAssociated Zone / Impact
Focus (Hypocenter)
View Answer
The exact subsurface point inside the Earth where stored potential energy from fault rupture is first released as elastic seismic waves
View Answer
Depth classification: Shallow (0–70 km), Intermediate (70–300 km), and Deep-focus (300–700 km / Wadati-Benioff Zone)
Epicentre
View Answer
The nearest point on the Earth's surface located vertically (90°) above the Focus where seismic waves arrive first
View Answer
Primary zone of maximum ground shaking, highest seismic intensity, and most severe structural damage on the surface
Primary Waves (P-Waves)
View Answer
Longitudinal / compressional waves (analogous to sound waves); travel fastest (6–13 km/s) through solid, liquid, and gaseous media
View Answer
First waves recorded on a seismograph; particles oscillate back and forth parallel to the direction of wave propagation
Secondary Waves (S-Waves)
View Answer
Transverse / shear waves (analogous to light waves); travel only through solids (3.5–7 km/s) and disappear completely in liquids
View Answer
Decisive scientific proof that the Outer Core is liquid; particles oscillate perpendicular (at right angles) to wave propagation
Surface Waves (L-Waves)
View Answer
Long-period slow waves travelling parallel to the ground surface from the epicentre (Rayleigh and Love waves); maximum amplitude
View Answer
Directly responsible for surface destruction, collapse of buildings, ground fissuring, and heavy loss of life and property
P-Wave Shadow Zone (105°–145°)
View Answer
A ring-shaped band between 105° and 145° angular distance from the epicentre where no direct P-waves are recorded
View Answer
Caused by sharp inward refraction and abrupt drop in wave velocity at the core-mantle boundary (Gutenberg Discontinuity)
S-Wave Shadow Zone (105°–180°)
View Answer
A vast hemispherical zone extending from 105° to the antipode (180°) from the epicentre where S-waves are completely absent
View Answer
Covers over 40% of the Earth's surface; caused by complete absorption of S-waves by the liquid Outer Core at 2,900 km depth

Important Facts & Contributions
Earthquakes Volcanoes 1
Seismic Belt / ScaleKey CharacteristicAssociated Regions / Details
Circum-Pacific Belt (Ring of Fire)
View Answer
Extensive chain of convergent and subduction boundaries encircling the Pacific Ocean; accounts for ~68% of the world's earthquakes
View Answer
Chile, Peru, Central America, California, Alaska, Aleutians, Japan, Philippines, Indonesia, and New Zealand
Mid-Continental Belt (Alpine–Himalayan)
View Answer
Fold mountain belt formed by compression and collision of the Eurasian, African, and Indian plates; accounts for ~21% of global earthquakes
View Answer
Alps, Carpathians, Caucasus, Zagros, Hindu Kush, Himalayas, and Myanmar ranges; destructive shallow- and intermediate-focus earthquakes
Mid-Atlantic Belt
View Answer
Sea floor spreading along divergent plate boundaries and transform faults; characterized by shallow-focus earthquakes
View Answer
Iceland, Azores, Saint Helena, Tristan da Cunha, and the axial rift valley of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge
Richter Scale
View Answer
Logarithmic scale developed by Charles F. Richter in 1935; measures total energy released at the earthquake source (Magnitude)
View Answer
Open-ended scale (1–9+); each whole-number step represents a 10-fold increase in wave amplitude and ~31.6 (32) times more energy
Mercalli Scale (MMI)
View Answer
Intensity scale developed by Giuseppe Mercalli in 1902; based on observed surface destruction, structural damage, and human perception
View Answer
Qualitative measurement in Roman numerals from I to XII (1 to 12); maximum at the epicentre and decreases outward with distance
Tsunami Waves
View Answer
Vertical displacement of ocean water by powerful submarine subduction earthquakes (>7.5 Mw), producing giant waves on shallow coasts
View Answer
26 December 2004 Sumatra Tsunami (Indian Ocean), 11 March 2011 Tohoku Tsunami (Japan Fukushima nuclear disaster)

What is the average velocity of P-waves in the Earth's crust?

What type of waves are P-waves, whose physical nature is analogous to sound waves?

In which direction do particles of the medium vibrate during the propagation of P-waves?

Tap to view answer
Through which states of matter can P-waves travel?
Through all three states: solid, liquid, and gas

Why do Secondary Waves (S-Waves) arrive at a seismograph after P-waves?
Because their velocity is about 40% slower than P-waves (approx. 3.5 to 5 km/second in the crust)

What type of waves are S-waves, whose physical nature is analogous to light waves?
Transverse or Shear Waves

In which direction do particles of the medium vibrate during the propagation of S-waves?
Perpendicular (at right angles, up-and-down or side-to-side) to the direction of wave propagation
Tap to view answer
What is the most important physical property of S-waves that became a milestone in understanding the Earth's interior?
They can propagate only through solid media and disappear completely in liquids or gases

How does the propagation velocity of L-waves compare with that of P and S waves?

How do the density and elasticity of rocks affect the velocity of seismic waves?

What is a Seismic Shadow Zone?

Between what angular distances from the epicentre is the P-wave Shadow Zone located?
