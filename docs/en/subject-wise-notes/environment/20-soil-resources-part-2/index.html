Section 1 — Soil Erosion: Definition, Causes, 4 Stages of Water Erosion & Chambal Ravines

What is the detachment and washing or blowing away of the most fertile upper layer (topsoil) of land by natural agents like water, wind, or glaciers and human activities called?
Soil Erosion
Tap to view answer
By what famous nickname is soil erosion known due to its destructive yet slow nature?
Creeping Death of the Soil
Tap to view answer
What is the slow rate at which soil erodes under natural vegetation cover while an equal amount of new soil continues to form called?
Geological or Normal Erosion
Tap to view answer
When human interference (deforestation, overgrazing, unscientific farming) makes the rate of soil erosion many times faster than its rate of formation, what is it called?
Accelerated Soil Erosion
Tap to view answer
Which is the largest and most prominent physical agent of soil erosion in India?
Water Erosion (monsoon rainfall and river runoff)
Tap to view answer
What are the four chronological stages of soil erosion caused by water?
1. Splash Erosion, 2. Sheet Erosion, 3. Rill Erosion, and 4. Gully Erosion
Tap to view answer
What is the first stage of water erosion, in which fast raindrops strike bare soil, splashing particles and breaking soil structure, called?
Raindrop or Splash Erosion
Tap to view answer
After heavy rainfall, when the most fertile topsoil of an entire field washes away invisibly as a uniform thin sheet with water, what is it called?
Sheet Erosion
Tap to view answer
Which type of water erosion is considered the 'most dangerous and insidious' for farmers because it is not immediately visible to the eye yet carries away all fertility?
Sheet Erosion
Tap to view answer
When runoff from sheet erosion flows down a slope and carves small, finger-like shallow channels in the soil (which can be leveled by normal ploughing), what is it called?
Rill Erosion
Tap to view answer
When the small channels of rill erosion deepen and widen into large trenches that cannot be crossed or leveled by ordinary farm implements, what is it called?
Gully Erosion
Tap to view answer
What is rugged, dissected land rendered completely unfit for agriculture due to severe gully erosion called?
Badland Topography or Ravines
Tap to view answer

Madhya Pradesh (Bhind, Morena, Sheopur), Rajasthan (Kota, Dholpur, Sawai Madhopur), and Uttar Pradesh (Agra, Etawah)

Landslide or Mass Wasting

What is the process of rivers cutting and collapsing the soil along their banks called?
Stream Bank Erosion
Tap to view answer
Which agricultural practice is a major anthropogenic cause of soil erosion in Northeast India (Assam, Meghalaya, Nagaland, Mizoram)?
Jhum Cultivation or Shifting Cultivation (Slash and Burn)
Tap to view answer
Why does deforestation accelerate soil erosion?
Because tree canopies can no longer break the velocity of raindrops and root binding is lost
Tap to view answer
What happens to soil when livestock graze beyond the carrying capacity of pastures (overgrazing)?
The protective grass cover is destroyed and animal hooves loosen the soil, exposing it to erosion
Tap to view answer
What damage is caused by ploughing up and down the slope?
Plough furrows become channels for water flow, causing severe rill and gully erosion
Tap to view answer
Who developed the Universal Soil Loss Equation (USLE: A = R × K × LS × C × P)?
Wischmeier and Smith
Tap to view answer
What do the factors 'R' and 'K' represent in the USLE equation?
R = Rainfall Erosivity and K = Soil Erodibility
Tap to view answer
Among silt, sand, and clay, which particle-size soil is most easily eroded by water (highest K-value)?
Silt and fine sand soils (because clay particles stick together and coarse sand is heavy)
Tap to view answer
When silt carried by soil erosion settles at the bottom of dams and reservoirs, what is this problem called?
Reservoir Siltation (which reduces water storage capacity and triggers floods)
Tap to view answer
What is the erosion of coastal land by sea waves and tides in coastal areas called?
Coastal or Marine Erosion
Tap to view answer
Which natural vegetation acts as the strongest bio-shield in preventing coastal erosion?
Mangrove Forests
Tap to view answer
What is the erosion caused by groundwater dissolving rocks in limestone (karst) regions called?
Chemical Corrosion or Karst Erosion
Tap to view answer
Approximately how many billion tonnes of fertile topsoil are washed away annually in India due to soil erosion?
About 5.3 billion (530 crore) tonnes per year
Tap to view answer
According to IIT Delhi and research studies, which region of India has the highest annual soil loss rate per hectare?
Brahmaputra Valley, Eastern Himalayas, and Shivalik Hills
Tap to view answer
Along with erosion, what chemical degradation of soil occurs in the Western Ghats and Northeast India due to heavy monsoon rainfall?
Intense Leaching and Acidification
Tap to view answer
What is the relationship between soil erosion and water conservation?
Both are two sides of the same coin — slowing down flowing water is key to holding the soil

Section 2 — Wind Erosion (Suspension, Saltation, Surface Creep), Desertification & UNCCD (LDN Target)

Which is the most prominent agent of soil erosion in arid and semi-arid regions (such as the Thar Desert of Rajasthan)?
Wind Erosion
Tap to view answer
Through how many primary physical processes does wind transport soil and sand particles during wind erosion?
3 processes: 1. Saltation, 2. Suspension, and 3. Surface Creep
Tap to view answer
In wind erosion, what is the bouncing movement of medium sand grains (0.1 to 0.5 mm diameter) in short leaps across the ground surface called?
Saltation
Tap to view answer
Which single process accounts for the largest share (about 50% to 75%) of total soil movement by wind?
Saltation
Tap to view answer
What is the lifting of ultra-fine dust and silt particles smaller than 0.1 mm high into strong winds to travel hundreds of kilometers as dust storms called?
Suspension
Tap to view answer
What is the rolling or sliding of coarse and heavy sand particles (0.5 to 2 mm diameter) along the ground due to impacts from saltating grains called?
Surface Creep (5–25% of total wind erosion)
Tap to view answer
What is the gradual conversion of fertile land into desert-like barren land in arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas due to climate change and human activities called?
Desertification
Tap to view answer
Does 'desertification' only mean the geographical expansion of existing deserts?
No; it means the persistent loss of biological productivity (land degradation) of any arid or semi-arid ecosystem
Tap to view answer
According to the 'Desertification and Land Degradation Atlas' (2021) released by ISRO's Space Applications Centre (SAC, Ahmedabad), what percentage of India's total geographical area is affected by land degradation?

Section 3 — Scientific Soil Conservation Techniques (Contour Ploughing, Terracing, Shelterbelts, Mulching, Zero Tillage) & Schemes

What is ploughing and sowing across a slope along lines of equal elevation (contour lines) rather than parallel to the slope called?
Contour Ploughing / Contour Farming
Tap to view answer
How does contour ploughing prevent soil erosion?
Each plough furrow acts as a small bund that slows down flowing water and allows it to infiltrate into the soil
Tap to view answer
What is cutting steep hill slopes (such as in Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, and Northeast India) into wide, flat, step-like fields for cultivation called?
Terrace Farming or Bench Terracing
Tap to view answer
What is building earthen or stone bunds along equal elevation lines on moderately sloping land to check water runoff called?
Contour Bunding
Tap to view answer
What is growing dense, soil-binding leguminous or grass crops (like moong, cowpea, soybean) in alternating strips between erosion-prone crops (like maize, cotton) called?
Strip Cropping
Tap to view answer
What is planting rows of trees and shrubs perpendicular to wind direction along field edges in desert and coastal areas to break wind speed called?
Shelterbelts and Windbreaks
Tap to view answer
What is the main technical difference between windbreaks and shelterbelts?
Windbreaks are small barriers of 1–2 rows, whereas shelterbelts are wide, multi-row triangular belts of trees and shrubs protecting larger areas
Tap to view answer
What is covering bare soil between crops with straw, dry leaves, crop residues, or plastic sheets to protect it from sun, wind, and direct raindrop impact called?
Mulching
Tap to view answer
What are the three biggest benefits of mulching?
1. Soil moisture conservation (checking evaporation), 2. Prevention of soil erosion, and 3. Weed control & temperature regulation
Tap to view answer
What are dense crops (such as cowpea, berseem, rye) grown after the main harvest to cover the soil and prevent erosion instead of leaving the field fallow called?
Cover Crops
Tap to view answer
What is the scientific practice of growing different crops in sequence on the same field (especially legumes after cereals) instead of monoculture called?
Crop Rotation
Tap to view answer
What is growing two or more crops simultaneously in the same field in a definite row pattern (such as wheat + mustard or sorghum + pigeon pea) called?
Intercropping
Tap to view answer
What is an integrated land-use system in which multipurpose trees and shrubs are grown alongside crops on agricultural land or field bunds called?
Agroforestry
Tap to view answer
In 2014, India became the first country in the world to adopt which policy?
National Agroforestry Policy, 2014
Tap to view answer
What is the modern conservation agriculture method of sowing the next crop directly into the stubble of the previous crop using a special seed drill without ploughing called?
Zero Tillage / No-Till Farming
Tap to view answer
What are the three core principles of 'Conservation Agriculture' promoted by the FAO?
1. Minimum or zero mechanical soil disturbance (Zero Tillage), 2. Permanent organic soil cover (Mulching/Crop Residue), and 3. Crop diversification / rotation
Tap to view answer
What are small stone, timber, or masonry barriers built across gullies to check gully erosion and trap silt to fill them up called?

What is Soil Erosion? Chambal Ravines, UNCCD & Soil Conservation Techniques

Soil erosion can strip away the fertile topsoil layer, impacting agriculture, water retention, and ecology. In this part, study sheet, rill, and gully erosion, the Chambal ravines, wind-driven soil transport, Land Degradation Neutrality, contour farming, terrace farming, shelterbelts, mulching, and zero tillage.

Key Topics Covered in This Chapter

  • Water Erosion and Chambal Ravines: Sheet, rill, and gully erosion and their impact on landforms.
  • Wind Erosion and Desertification: Suspension, saltation, and surface creep, along with soil loss in arid regions.
  • Agricultural Techniques for Soil Conservation: Contour farming, terrace farming, mulching, crop rotation, strip cropping, cover crops, and zero tillage.
  • UNCCD, LDN, and Land Management: Land Degradation Neutrality, the Bonn Challenge, watershed management, and soil/water conservation schemes.

Frequently Asked Questions by Students

What are the main types of soil erosion? Water erosion includes processes such as sheet, rill, and gully erosion; in wind erosion, particles can move through suspension, saltation, and surface creep.

How do contour farming and terrace farming help in soil conservation? They can slow down flowing water and soil runoff on slopes, helping reduce erosion and conserve moisture.
