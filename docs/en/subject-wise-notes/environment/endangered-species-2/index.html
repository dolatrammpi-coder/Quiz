Endangered Species of India and Conservation Measures

Measures adopted for conserving endangered wildlife in India include habitat protection, protected area management, curbing poaching and illegal trade, species-specific programmes, and community participation. The effectiveness of conservation depends not only on population numbers of a species, but also on habitat quality, genetic diversity, breeding success, and management of human-wildlife conflict.

Key Threats and Their Drivers

Habitat fragmentation and destruction can be the greatest pressure for many species. Roads, mining, dams, agricultural expansion, and unplanned construction can fragment habitats. Poaching/illegal trade, pollution, invasive species, disease, climate change, and human-wildlife conflict also escalate risks. Conservation plans must be tailored to local conditions and the ecology of the species.

  • In-situ pressures: habitat loss, fragmentation, poaching, and conflict.
  • Risks associated with ex-situ: limits on genetic diversity and reintroduction.
  • Counting numbers alone is not enough; also assess habitat quality and breeding.

In-situ and Ex-situ Conservation

In-situ conservation protects species within their natural habitats, such as National Parks, Wildlife Sanctuaries, and other effective protected areas. Ex-situ conservation can involve management outside natural habitats, such as zoos, seed/germplasm banks, captive breeding, and, under certain conditions, reintroduction programmes. Both approaches are complementary, though not a substitute for habitat conservation.

  • In-situ: conservation in natural habitats.
  • Ex-situ: controlled conservation/breeding outside natural habitats.
  • Reintroduction requires suitable habitat, health screening, and long-term monitoring.

Conservation Programmes and Communities

Programmes like Project Tiger and Project Elephant highlight the need for habitat and landscape management alongside species protection. Compensation for local communities, safe corridors, early warning systems, alternative livelihoods, and public awareness can help reduce human-wildlife conflict. Programmes should be evaluated using evidence-based monitoring and independent data.

  • Wildlife corridors can aid movement and gene flow between populations.
  • Local participation can reduce illegal activities and conflict.
  • Species-specific plans should account for habitat, diet, breeding, and disease risks.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between in-situ and ex-situ conservation?

In-situ is conservation within natural habitats; ex-situ is controlled conservation or breeding outside natural habitats.

Why are wildlife corridors necessary?

They connect habitats to support animal movement, breeding, and gene flow between populations.

What is the role of communities in conserving endangered species?

Local monitoring, alternative livelihoods, conflict mitigation, traditional knowledge, and participation in conservation decisions can improve outcomes.
