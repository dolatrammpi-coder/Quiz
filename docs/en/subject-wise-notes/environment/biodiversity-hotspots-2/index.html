Four Biodiversity Hotspots of India

India's four biodiversity hotspots, their geographical locations, characteristic species, threats to habitats, and conservation measures form the core of this chapter. Instead of memorizing only their names, understand which region corresponds to the Himalayas, the Northeast, the Western Ghats, or the Nicobar Islands.

Four Regions Related to India

  • The Himalaya hotspot is linked to the Indian Himalayan region, featuring altitudinal forests, grasslands, and alpine ecosystems. The Indo-Burma hotspot encompasses the biodiversity of several parts of Northeast India.
  • In the Western Ghats–Sri Lanka hotspot, the moist forest systems and endemic species of the Western Ghats are prominent. The Indian portion of the Sundaland hotspot pertains to the Nicobar Islands; these four regions are not identical geographical units.

Biological Uniqueness and Endemism

  • Numerous endemic amphibians, plants, and other fauna are found in the Western Ghats. In the Himalayas, variations in altitude and climate create diverse habitats.
  • The rivers, forests, hills, and rainfall of Northeast India are vital for biodiversity. When studying examples of species, verify their geographical distribution and conservation status separately.

Major Threats to Hotspot Regions

  • Conversion of forests, infrastructure such as roads and dams, mining, unplanned tourism, forest fires, poaching, and invasive species can fragment habitats.
  • Climate change can alter temperature, rainfall, and species distribution. Endemic species living in small, isolated habitats can be particularly vulnerable.

Practical Conservation Measures

  • Effective management of protected areas, protection of wildlife corridors, respect for the rights and livelihoods of local communities, habitat restoration, and biodiversity monitoring are key measures.
  • Hotspot status does not automatically make an area a legally protected zone. National laws, state-level management, community-based initiatives, and cross-border cooperation each play a distinct role in conservation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which are the four hotspots linked to India?

Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats–Sri Lanka, and Sundaland. In India, the relevant part of Sundaland is the Nicobar Islands.

Is every biodiversity hotspot a legally protected area?

No. A hotspot is a biogeographical concept for conservation priority; legal protected area status is granted through a separate statutory process.

Why is endemism important in hotspots?

Endemic species have a restricted global distribution, so the destruction of their local habitats can sharply increase the risk of extinction.
