Biodiversity Hotspots: Definition and Criteria

What a biodiversity hotspot is, what criteria are used to identify it, and which global hotspot regions India is connected to—questions on these topics require clarity on the definition, geographical extent, and the meaning of endemism. A hotspot is not merely a species-rich region, but one with high endemism and severe habitat degradation.

Concept of Biodiversity Hotspots

  • A hotspot is a biogeographical unit that hosts numerous endemic plant and animal species, yet a large proportion of its natural habitat has been destroyed or altered.
  • This concept helps prioritize limited conservation resources in regions where endemic biodiversity is exceptionally high and extinction pressure is severe.

Criteria for Hotspots

  • According to the criteria popularized by Conservation International, a region must contain at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species, representing at least 0.5 percent of the global total.
  • The second criterion is the loss of at least 70 percent of its original natural vegetation. Remember both conditions for exams; high species richness alone is not sufficient to qualify as a hotspot.

Global Significance of Hotspots

  • Hotspot boundaries are defined on the basis of ecological regions rather than national borders alone. A single hotspot may span multiple countries.
  • Habitat destruction, land-use change, poaching, invasive species, and climate change threaten many hotspot regions. Both habitat protection and local community participation are essential for conservation.

Biodiversity Hotspots Related to India

  • India's landmass is associated with the Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats–Sri Lanka, and Sundaland hotspots. In India, the Sundaland region is primarily linked to the Nicobar Islands.
  • In the Himalayas and Western Ghats, habitats vary with altitude, rainfall, and topography; Northeast India is connected to the biological diversity of the Indo-Burma region. Practice locating these regions and their boundary transitions on a map.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the two conditions for becoming a biodiversity hotspot?

Having at least 1,500 endemic species of vascular plants and having lost at least 70 percent of the original natural vegetation are the primary criteria.

How many biodiversity hotspots are recognized in India?

India is linked to parts of four hotspot regions—Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats–Sri Lanka, and Sundaland.

What does endemic species mean?

An endemic species is one whose natural distribution is restricted to a particular geographical area and is not naturally found everywhere.
